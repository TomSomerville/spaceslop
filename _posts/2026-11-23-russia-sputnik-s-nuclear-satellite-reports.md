Western intelligence agencies believe Russia has finished building a satellite carrying a nuclear charge and could put it into orbit as soon as December, according to a joint investigation by German public broadcasters WDR and NDR and the newspaper Süddeutsche Zeitung, published Nov. 23 and carried by Tagesschau. The spacecraft is reportedly called Sputnik-S and has allegedly sat in storage near Moscow for several years.

None of this is confirmed. It is an intelligence assessment relayed through journalists, and the accounts here come from secondary reports of the German investigation. Russia has not acknowledged any such satellite.

## What the reporting says

According to the coverage, warnings circulated among NATO members in early November that the device could be launched within 60 days. Germany's Bundeswehr Space Command reportedly assesses that a launch could come as early as December and that the satellite could become operational shortly after reaching orbit. The Bundeswehr and Germany's Federal Intelligence Service, the BND, have recently shared related information with several allies, per the same reporting.

The investigation also revisits Kosmos 2553, launched in February 2022 into an unusual orbit. Moscow describes it as a platform for studying cosmic radiation; Western officials have suggested it may have been used to test how satellite electronics hold up against intense radiation.

Germany's defense ministry declined to answer specific questions, citing the confidentiality of internal discussions. The Russian embassy said it had no information about the events described, rejected the claims as part of a "propaganda campaign," and said Russia's space activities are peaceful and comply with international law.

## Why the industry should care

The suspected weapon is not aimed at the ground. A nuclear detonation in orbit would act on everything nearby through radiation, debris and electromagnetic effects. US Space Command chief Gen. Stephen Whiting has previously said such a blast could disable about a third of satellites in low Earth orbit within hours or days, with more failing over the following months. German officers quoted in the reporting also warned of knock-on effects on the ground, including power outages and degraded communications and navigation.

That is a direct risk to the commercial constellations that now dominate LEO: broadband networks, Earth observation fleets and the military systems built on commercial buses. Unlike a kinetic anti-satellite test, which damages one target and leaves a debris field, a high-altitude nuclear burst would not discriminate between owners or nationalities.

## Context

The idea is not new. US officials raised concerns about a Russian nuclear anti-satellite program in 2024, and an October 2026 US Defense Intelligence Agency space-security assessment said Russia is developing a satellite to carry a nuclear weapon, while noting that few effective countermeasures would exist once it was in orbit. Placing weapons of mass destruction in orbit is banned under the 1967 Outer Space Treaty. What is new in this report is the claimed name, the claimed status of the hardware, and a timeline measured in weeks.
