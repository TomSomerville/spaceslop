The race to land the proposed U.S. Space Academy now has a field: NASA Deputy Administrator Matt Anderson said on X that 25 states submitted proposals before the bidding window closed. The deadline had been pushed back from its original Oct. 26 date.

NASA has not published the full list of applicants. Stars and Stripes identified 15 of them: Alabama, Arizona, California, Colorado, Florida, Indiana, Louisiana, Maryland, Nevada, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Tennessee, Texas, Virginia and West Virginia. Anderson called the volume of bids "a clear sign of the national interest" in the project. Local reports of other states pitching sites, Washington among them, were not confirmed by the sources read for this piece.

## What is being built

President Trump signed an executive order on Aug. 28 creating the academy as a civilian, NASA-led counterpart to the military service academies. The order set up a commission, which includes Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and Air Force Secretary Troy Meink, to settle curriculum, service obligations and how the school gets established. Stars and Stripes reports the commission's recommendations are due to the president on Dec. 26.

NASA's September request for information laid out the site criteria, as Payload reported: a permanent campus of at least 300 acres, room for 1,200 undergraduates and the ability to scale to 4,000. Proximity to NASA or university facilities is a plus rather than a requirement. Each state could submit one proposal through its governor's designee covering location, timeline, cost and partners. The stated schedule is groundbreaking by 2027, an interim cohort of 300 students at partner institutions in 2028, and a permanent campus by 2031.

## Not a done deal

Congress would still have to pass legislation to create a service academy, so a White House site decision alone does not establish the school. The bids also come with politics attached. Colorado, home to the Air Force Academy and several Space Force installations, is bidding even though Sen. John Hickenlooper has called the plan "duplicative, costly, and unserious." Gen. Douglas Schiess, the Space Force's chief of space operations, has said the service will keep commissioning about 100 Guardians a year from the Air Force Academy regardless.

The state pitches lean on existing space infrastructure. Payload's earlier survey had Texas building a bid around Johnson Space Center, Florida around Kennedy Space Center and the Cape, and Alabama around Marshall and Redstone Arsenal. Nevada's proposed spaceport developer offered a 240-acre parcel, below NASA's 300-acre minimum but next to roughly 100,000 acres of federal land.

For the launch and spacecraft industry, the academy is a long-range talent pipeline story, and the Dec. 26 report is the next date to watch.
