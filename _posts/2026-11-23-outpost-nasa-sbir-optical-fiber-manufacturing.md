Outpost Technologies, the Playa Vista, California company building return-from-orbit cargo vehicles, says NASA has selected it for two Phase I awards aimed at making optical fiber in microgravity. The awards come through NASA's Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) and Small Business Technology Transfer (STTR) programs. Outpost's announcement doesn't give the dollar value or a schedule for Phase II.

## Two tracks

The SBIR effort is about material purity. Outpost describes a two-stage process that first purifies binary metal fluoride precursors, then melts the glass and forms preforms (the glass rods from which fiber is drawn) in microgravity. The purification target is no more than 10 parts per billion for each 3d transition metal and for O-H impurities. Phase I work covers designing the purification process, building a ground-based melt baseline and defining a payload concept compatible with the ISS.

The STTR effort is an Automated Orbital Fiber Puller, an autonomous system for the ISS or commercial low Earth orbit platforms. Its purpose is to remove crew from the loop: no loading preforms, threading fiber, recovering breaks or handling spools. Outpost is developing it with Indiana University's FAMES Lab, led by Alexander Gumennik, with input from Kenneth Levin. Phase I covers modeling, process controls, a preliminary architecture and a roadmap to Phase II.

## Why ZBLAN

The target material is ZBLAN, a fluoride glass fiber. Outpost points to a theoretical minimum loss of about 0.02 dB/km, compared with 0.14 dB/km for silica at 1550 nm, and says the work is aimed at photonic quantum computers. The long-running argument for making it in orbit is that microgravity suppresses the crystallization that degrades the fiber on the ground; Outpost's release frames the program around purity and automation instead.

Outpost says StarFiber is planned as the first product from its Space Factories. Phase I awards are small and early-stage, so this is a signal of NASA interest rather than a commitment to flight hardware. The automation piece may be the more consequential one: a fiber puller that needs no crew time is far easier to sell to a commercial station than one that eats astronaut hours.
