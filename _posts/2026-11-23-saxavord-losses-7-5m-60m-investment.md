SaxaVord Spaceport, the Shetland launch site run by Shetland Space Centre, lost more than £7.5m before tax in 2025, according to accounts filed at Companies House and reported by the Shetland Times. The loss compares with £5.4m a year earlier, even as revenue rose by more than a quarter to £3.19m.

The pre-tax figure was £7.56m. Financial costs climbed from £880,000 to £2.27m, and staffing costs rose from £3.9m to £4.4m as headcount grew from 70 to 78. The top director's pay fell from £213,000 to £188,000.

## A balance sheet reset after year end

The accounts predate an October investment of £60m from private and public backers. The UK government took a significant stake, and private investor Wild Ventures Ltd put in £30m. As part of the deal, £33.6m in loan notes and accrued interest was converted into shares, which the company says leaves the group debt-free. It described the money as a "significant statement of confidence."

According to the directors' report, the funding is meant to complete all three launchpads, add client facilities and cover operating losses until launches become profitable.

## Still waiting on a first launch

The report was signed on 9 November. At that point, two launch service providers were working toward a launch before the end of 2026. A launch that had been proposed for summer 2026 was cancelled after Rocket Factory Augsburg reported an issue following what the paper describes as a successful week of testing. The directors called future flights "the first of many launches from SaxaVord Spaceport." The Times report does not say which providers are targeting the end-of-year slot, and the article was cut off in the version available to us, so details beyond the above are unconfirmed.

Hangar integration facilities and cleanrooms are being built for clients launching from 2027.

## Side income

Unlike some European rival spaceports, SaxaVord is earning money away from rockets. Accounts show £450,012 from accommodation in Haroldswick and £295,683 from data services at a data hub in Baltasound. Those lines are small against the loss, but they are steady revenue at a site that has yet to host an orbital attempt.

The pattern is familiar for new spaceports: costs arrive years before launch fees do. With the debt cleared and capital in hand, the open question is whether a first launch lands before the end of 2026 or slips into next year.
