The UK government has set the liability limit at £0 for orbital operator licences covering in-orbit servicing, assembly and manufacturing (ISAM) and lunar missions, with the change taking effect for licences issued on or after 1 December 2026. Operators of those missions will not need third-party liability insurance for the licensed activity.

The waiver applies to planned launches before the end of 2030, including ones that slip past that date, and the government says it will review the policy in 2029. It covers only ISAM and lunar missions for now; other mission types may be considered after further review. Launch procurement still carries its own liability and insurance requirements.

## Variable limits for everyone else

For other orbital operators, the government is replacing a flat approach with variable liability limits, applying to applications submitted from 1 December under either the Space Industry Act 2018 or the Outer Space Act 1986. The default limit written into licences will be £50 million. Operators can bring it down to £20 million by meeting one of two criteria, or to £0 by meeting both.

The two criteria are security and sustainability. The first means taking part in, and being audited under, UK Space Agency-funded cyber and physical security assessments. The second means having access to current sustainability information such as the ISO 24113 debris-mitigation standard. Submitting evidence is voluntary; the UK Space Agency assesses it and the Civil Aviation Authority forwards it. A technical framework is due before 1 December, with a further iteration expected in late 2027 or early 2028.

The government response notes that it did not go ahead with an earlier idea to abolish liability requirements altogether in favour of the variable-limit approach. That is as far as the document goes on the earlier proposal; it does not elaborate here.

## Licensing housekeeping

The CAA is updating its liability and insurance guidance before the changes start. Limits in licences will be denominated in pounds sterling rather than euros for applications from 1 December. The regulator will take a more proportionate approach to deposit and security requirements, and existing decommissioning funds held for constellation operators will be wound up, with the CAA and BIST contacting affected operators.

The CAA and the UK Space Agency are also developing a streamlined approach that would authorise multiple missions under a single licence. How application fees are treated will be settled within that project.

## Why it matters

Insurance cost is a real barrier for servicing and lunar startups, whose missions involve close approaches to other objects that insurers find hard to price. A zero cap removes that line item for a defined window, which should help UK-linked servicers and lunar operators compete with jurisdictions that are lighter touch. The 2030 cut-off and 2029 review mean it is a trial rather than a permanent settlement.
