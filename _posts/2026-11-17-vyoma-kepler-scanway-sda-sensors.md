Vyoma, the Munich space domain awareness company, has signed contracts with Toronto-based Kepler Communications and Polish optics firm Scanway to put its surveillance sensors on ten of Kepler's Tranche 2 satellites. The announcement came on 17 November.

Under the deal, Vyoma will fly multiple FlaHOP (Flamingo Hosted Payload) sensors on the Kepler spacecraft, which are scheduled to launch no earlier than October 2028 on a dedicated rocket. Scanway co-developed the sensors with Vyoma. Financial terms were not disclosed.

## What it adds up to

The plan is to build a "space fence" in a dusk-dawn orbit, a geometry that lets the sensors cover objects several times a day. Kepler's optical data-relay network will carry the observations back, which Vyoma says will allow near-real-time reporting of anomalies, satellite manoeuvres and newly launched objects. The data will also feed an independent commercial catalogue of space objects, which Vyoma plans to make commercially available from early 2029.

Each payload includes Vyoma's own on-board computer, which handles instrument control, image compression, processing and encryption. If needed, the system can cue Vyoma's Flamingo satellites and other sensors for follow-up observations. Flamingo-1 has been on orbit since early 2026, and Flamingo-2 is scheduled to launch in spring 2027.

## Context

The deal extends an earlier 2026 agreement under which one Kepler satellite will host two FlaHOP sensors for ESA's HydRON Element #3 programme. Kepler says it has launched 33 satellites to date.

Hosting sensors on a communications constellation is a cost-sharing approach to space surveillance: the host provides the platform and the data link, and the sensor operator avoids building and launching its own dedicated fleet. For Vyoma, that means ten additional vantage points without ten additional launches of its own.

Vyoma's Jens Utzmann called the setup a "strategic gamechanger" for European sovereignty in space domain awareness, a description that is the company's own. Scanway CEO Jędrzej Kowalewski said there is no safe orbit without good monitoring. Kepler's Michael Dowell said hosting the sensors enables a more responsive approach to monitoring low Earth orbit.

The timeline is long: nothing flies before late 2028, and the commercial catalogue is not due until 2029. Until then, the announcement is a commitment rather than a capability.
