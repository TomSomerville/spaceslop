Elon Musk and Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy are trading public barbs over whether the FAA should be running Starlink, days after a nationwide ground stop exposed how brittle the agency's legacy communications network is.

According to Teslarati's account, Musk posted on X on Monday that free Starlink terminals had been offered to Duffy for the FAA and that Duffy said they were not needed. He later answered Duffy's own remarks with a facepalm emoji.

Duffy did not deny an offer was made. At a press conference he said Starlink had helped with some FAA assets in Alaska, but that an earlier version sent to the agency's technical lab ran into problems, and the FAA went with a ground-based network instead. He did not say what testing found. He called Starlink's speed "remarkable" but argued the country's airspace is too large to depend on a single provider.

## The backdrop

The spat follows the November 13 ground stop, when the FAA halted departures nationwide after legacy equipment became unstable under a flood of network connection attempts. Per the report, the agency traced the flood to a radar site in Amarillo, Texas, and a radio site in El Dorado, Arkansas, where L3Harris was installing a new router. About 12,462 flights were canceled that day.

Duffy said he wants to move away from L3Harris, which operates the current network, but cannot cancel its contract. He has asked Congress for $50 billion to modernize air traffic control.

## Old argument

None of this is new. In a July 2025 letter, FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said the agency would not use Starlink, or any satellite service, as the sole communications technology for safety-critical services. Reuters reported at the time that the FAA was not considering swapping out its L3Harris and Verizon contracts for Starlink; the Verizon deal, awarded in 2023, is worth up to $2.4 billion over 15 years. In early 2025 SpaceX said it was supplying Starlink kits to the FAA at no cost and did not intend to take over any existing contract.

For SpaceX, a federal air traffic role would be a prestige customer for Starlink's growing enterprise business. For the FAA, the practical question is redundancy: Bedford's letter and Duffy's comments both point to a mixed architecture rather than a single-vendor bet. This account rests on one outlet's reporting of Musk's post and Duffy's remarks.
