Mexican authorities are pressing SpaceX to take responsibility for suspected rocket debris that has been washing ashore at Bagdad Beach, near Matamoros in Tamaulipas, since around Nov. 17. The fragments are believed to come from the Super Heavy booster that broke up over the Gulf of Mexico on the Nov. 13 Starship test flight, after an aborted catch attempt in which its engines stopped firing too soon.

## What has been found

The environmental group Conibio Global says it has collected at least 1,500 fragments, and its president, Jesús Elías Ibarra, says there are thousands. The group describes white plastic with gray tape, black rubber tape and blue adhesive, which it believes came from the booster's internal lining, though it says the origin still needs confirmation. It also reports that the debris was mixed with ordinary tourist waste. A Caguama turtle was found dead nearby, and environmental groups are carrying out a necropsy to see whether the debris played any part. No link has been established.

Profepa, Mexico's federal environmental protection agency, told the public not to touch suspected fragments and to report them. It is investigating SpaceX's possible responsibility, federal agencies have begun collecting debris, and the Navy is conducting maritime and land surveillance, according to Mexico News Daily.

## SpaceX's response

In a social media statement, SpaceX said its contacts in the Mexican government had found "very few" pieces of rocket debris and that it was unclear whether they came from SpaceX at all. It called most posts claiming widespread debris "fabrications or deliberately intended to deceive" and said debris from its booster poses no chemical, biological or toxicological risk.

## A repeat problem

This is not the first time. Debris from an earlier Starship loss reached the Tamaulipas coast last year, and President Claudia Sheinbaum threatened legal action before ruling out a lawsuit in July and opting for talks with the United States. Mexico News Daily notes a similar incident in October, following a Sept. 28 launch. Starbase sits about two miles north of the border, with Bagdad Beach roughly another mile south.

The dispute adds to a growing list of local friction around the Texas site, including an environmental challenge to a land swap that gave SpaceX 715 acres of wildlife refuge, which a federal judge declined to block in September. Fragment counts and arrival dates differ between outlets, so the numbers should be treated as preliminary.
