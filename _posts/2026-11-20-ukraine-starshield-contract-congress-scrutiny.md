Ukraine's military satellite connectivity, supplied by SpaceX's Starshield service, has become a congressional oversight target and a contract negotiation at the same time. Ukraine's chargé d'affaires in Washington, Denys Sienik, said negotiations with the Pentagon on a successor to the current Starshield contract are only just beginning, according to Breaking Defense. The existing deal expires in mid-2027.

## The October disruption

Starshield is the military variant of Starlink, and Ukraine relies on it to operate drones against Russian targets. In mid-October, Ukrainian officials said a sudden change to the network caused drone and defense failures that contributed to military and civilian deaths. The Hill reported that Ukraine said it had abruptly lost the ability to use Starshield, and that Starlink was also affected.

Nobody has said publicly why. Trump and Elon Musk have both denied causing the outage, according to The Hill, and the piece names no US official who limited the service. Breaking Defense notes the open question is whether it was a technical glitch or a decision by someone in the US government or at SpaceX. Sienik said Ukraine still has no answer from the Pentagon or SpaceX, though he described the system as working at the time of his remarks and said he does not want a repeat.

## Congress weighs in

Rep. Brian Fitzpatrick, a Pennsylvania Republican, said in a post on X that anyone limiting Starshield for Ukraine would face investigation, including public oversight hearings, and promised "immediate harsh and punitive budget cuts to any responsible agency or agency head." The threat is aimed at government agencies, not at SpaceX directly. Sen. Richard Blumenthal said senators of both parties were preparing to act.

Breaking Defense adds that Democrats, poised to win both chambers in the midterms by its reading, are expected to hold oversight hearings that could cover Starshield.

## Hedging on a single supplier

Sienik called Starshield "no effective alternative" for low-Earth-orbit military communications, but said Ukraine is looking at short- and long-term options, including domestic development and European systems being built by partners. For SpaceX, the episode is a reminder that its government business carries political risk alongside its technical lead: customers who depend on one provider for a battlefield capability will want a backup, and Congress now wants to know who can switch it off.

SpaceX had not commented in the coverage reviewed here.
