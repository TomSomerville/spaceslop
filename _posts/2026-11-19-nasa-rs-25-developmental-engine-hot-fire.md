NASA fired a developmental RS-25 engine for its full planned duration of 200 seconds on Wednesday, November 18, at Stennis Space Center near Bay St. Louis, Mississippi. The agency published its account of the test on November 19.

The engine, designated No. 10001, was run on the Fred Haise Test Stand. NASA says it will not fly on an Artemis mission. The point of the exercise is data: the agency and its prime engine contractor, L3Harris Technologies, use developmental units to learn how to improve performance and reliability of the engines that will. Test operators came from NASA, L3Harris and Nova Space Solutions.

## Why a non-flight engine matters

The RS-25 is the engine family that powers the core stage of the Space Launch System. Four of them together produce up to 2 million pounds of thrust on each Artemis launch, according to NASA. Every flight engine is tested at Stennis before it is cleared to fly, and the agency's line is that all RS-25s are "tested and proven flightworthy" there before use.

Developmental firings like this one sit alongside that acceptance testing. They let engineers push on design changes and gather performance data without putting a flight-designated engine at risk, which matters for a program that will be buying and flying these engines for years.

## What's next

NASA's release points to Artemis IV, the planned crewed Moon landing, which the agency lists for 2028, with SLS launching the Orion spacecraft from Kennedy Space Center. The release does not say what changes, if any, this particular test was evaluating, nor does it report any anomalies or specific performance figures beyond the successful full-duration burn.
