Raytheon has awarded a joint contract to Ursa Major and Avio USA to design, develop and qualify a new solid rocket motor booster for the Tomahawk cruise missile, according to a press release published by Ursa Major on Wednesday. The award gives both companies a path to becoming qualified suppliers on the Navy program.

Raytheon, an RTX business, builds the Tomahawk for the U.S. Navy. The company describes the dual-source approach as a way to add competition and production flexibility and to shore up the supply chain for the weapon. Sam Deneke, president of Naval Power at Raytheon, said in the release that expanding the industrial base for Tomahawk propulsion is essential to keeping the capability ready for the fleet.

## Who does what

Avio USA, headquartered in Arlington, Virginia, brings solid rocket propulsion heritage and manufacturing expertise. Ursa Major, based in Berthoud, Colorado, with manufacturing in Youngstown, Ohio, contributes its design approach and advanced manufacturing processes. The release calls it a multi-year program. The next step is to qualify the jointly designed booster and demonstrate eligibility for future production orders.

The release gives no contract value, quantities or schedule milestones, so the size of the award is unknown.

## Why space watchers should care

Solid rocket motors are the bottleneck shared by missiles and launch vehicles, and the same few suppliers feed both markets. Newer entrants such as Ursa Major have been building capacity in tactical missile motors; the company's earlier Navy work is covered in our report on its [$137 million in Navy motor contracts](/2026/10/05/ursa-major-navy-137m-solid-rocket-motors/). Avio USA, the American arm of the Italian propulsion group, broke ground in September on a new solid rocket motor plant in southern Virginia, per the release.

Avio is also in the news elsewhere: Iceye has bid for the parent company, a story we have followed in [Iceye bids for Avio](/2026/11/17/iceye-bids-for-avio/). Jason Meredith, Ursa Major's president, framed the Tomahawk work as ensuring the warfighter has the munitions needed, when needed.
