China launched a satellite called Shijian-24 on a Long March 2D rocket from Launch Complex 9 at the Taiyuan Satellite Launch Center at 6:51 a.m. China Standard Time on Nov. 19 (22:51 UTC on Nov. 18). Chinese state media, as relayed by Ratopati, described the mission as completely successful, and Interesting Engineering reports the spacecraft reached its planned sun-synchronous orbit.

That is about all officials have said. Neither the satellite's purpose nor its manufacturer has been disclosed. Sun-synchronous orbits suit Earth observation and other missions that need consistent lighting, but China has not confirmed that Shijian-24 will do either. The Long March 2D's 3.8-meter fairing hints at a sizeable payload envelope, though that alone says little about the spacecraft.

## What the numbers say

By Interesting Engineering's count, this was the 678th flight of the Long March family, the 108th of the Long March 2D variant, the 284th Long March mission for the Shanghai Academy of Spaceflight Technology, which develops the rocket, and China's 82nd orbital launch attempt of 2026.

The Shijian name translates roughly to "Practice" and is used for spacecraft that test technologies and establish operating procedures. The same outlet notes that earlier Shijian flights this year included Shijian-31, placed in a highly elliptical orbit in June, and a Shijian-32 launch attempt in January that failed.

## Cold weather at Taiyuan

Interesting Engineering also reports that cold conditions required extra thermal insulation on the rocket and heating of critical sections before liftoff. It adds that this Long March 2D did not carry the flight computer improvements and fin removal seen on another rocket a week earlier. China did not explain the difference; the outlet speculates it may relate to an older production batch, which officials have not confirmed.

Opaque launches of this kind are routine for China's Shijian and Shiyan series. Without an official payload description, outside observers will have to wait for orbital tracking data to infer what the satellite is meant to do.
