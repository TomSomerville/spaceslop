Japanese lunar company ispace and telecommunications group NTT signed a collaboration agreement on November 16 to develop communications and power infrastructure for future Moon missions. The two plan joint research, development and demonstrations, with commercialization as the eventual goal.

The work covers two areas: high-speed optical communications between Earth and the Moon, and laser-based power transmission on the lunar surface. NTT brings technology from its IOWN (Innovative Optical and Wireless Network) initiative, a light-based networking effort, along with its Space Integrated Computing Network concept and wireless power work. ispace contributes experience in lunar transportation, exploration and mission operations with its landers and rovers.

## What is actually agreed

This is a study agreement, not a hardware contract. The companies say they will begin by examining use cases, system architectures, technical requirements and demonstration methods, with possible phased demonstrations on Earth and in space as lunar transport and development activity progresses. Financial terms and a schedule for NTT's deliverables were not disclosed.

## The rationale

ispace's release argues that growing lunar activity, from resource exploration and science to construction and transport, will generate large volumes of data. It also says Japan will need sovereign communications capabilities for safe lunar operations, space traffic management and space situational awareness. On the power side, it notes the Moon has no existing power distribution infrastructure, which creates demand for flexible delivery to mobile equipment such as rovers and construction machinery.

ispace founder and CEO Takeshi Hakamada said in the release that sustainable lunar activities will require "not only transportation capabilities to reach the Moon." Minako Tsumenaga, an NTT senior vice president, described the aim as "a new infrastructure that connects the Earth and the Moon through light."

## Context

The deal fits ispace's push beyond landers into services. The company is planning Mission 3, targeting a lunar landing in 2028. In March 2026 it announced plans to commercialize a Lunar Connect Service built on a constellation of lunar-orbit satellites, and its release says the first lunar-orbiting satellite could launch as part of Mission 2.5 as early as 2027. An optical partner with a large terrestrial research base gives that plan a credible technology source, though nothing here commits either company to flight hardware.

Lunar relay and power infrastructure is also an area where NASA's lunar program and several commercial players are positioning. An agreement like this one tells us where ispace thinks its business goes after the landing: selling data and positioning as well as rides.
