The Federal Communications Commission said Wednesday it is authorizing SpaceX to deploy up to one million satellites for orbital data centers, a constellation SpaceX calls Starmind. Reuters reported the decision, and the FCC described it as the first US authorization for a constellation designed to process data in orbit.

The million-satellite figure is a ceiling on the approval, not a build order. SpaceX first filed for the system in January, asking for authority to operate what it described as orbital compute modules that would handle large volumes of data for use on Earth. The February application laid out satellites spread across orbits roughly 500 to 2,000 kilometers up, powered by solar arrays, with laser links to pass data between spacecraft and radio links for tracking and emergency commands. SpaceX later showed how satellites could fly in clusters to work together, according to Tesla North's account of the decision.

## What is and isn't settled

Starmind is separate from the Starlink internet constellation, though the application described possible links between the two networks. Neither report I read listed license conditions, and SpaceX has not published launch details beyond Elon Musk's own estimate. Speaking at the Baron Investment Conference, Musk said the first Starmind computers could launch in the third quarter of 2027, with larger deployments in following years, and called the date a guess. SpaceX has been working with Nvidia on the hardware, per Tesla North. One aggregator also reports the FCC will review further technical filings on December 15; that date comes from a single outlet and is unconfirmed.

The timeline fits earlier signals. SpaceX's CFO recently pointed to 2027 for orbital compute, which we covered in [SpaceX CFO outlines orbital compute timeline](/2026/11/23/spacex-cfo-orbital-compute-starmind-2027/).

## Why it matters

An FCC grant is permission, not proof the economics work. The agency's approval clears a regulatory hurdle for a plan that skeptics, including AWS chief executive Matt Garman earlier this year, have called far from reality, and astronomers have raised concerns about orbital crowding and brightness. A constellation at this scale would also depend on Starship flying at a cadence it has not yet demonstrated, since the original filing pointed to the reusable rocket as the launch vehicle.

Still, the decision gives SpaceX something competitors working on orbital compute, from startups to Amazon and Blue Origin, do not yet have: a US license for a system of this size. Whether it turns into hardware depends on launch capacity, chip supply and whether orbital compute can beat terrestrial data centers on cost.
