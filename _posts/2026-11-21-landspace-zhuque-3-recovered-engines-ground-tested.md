Chinese launch company LandSpace says the nine liquid oxygen–methane engines from the first stage it recovered in August have been torn down, refurbished and fired again on the ground. The claim comes from a company executive, and the supporting detail published so far is thin.

According to a report carried by iFeng, which credits IT之家 and CCTV Finance, LandSpace vice president Zhang Jingru disclosed the milestone on November 18 at an APEC finance event. She said all nine engines from the Zhuque-3 Y2 first stage were disassembled, inspected, repaired and reassembled, and have since completed ground acceptance and test firing.

## Why it matters

Landing a booster is only half of reusability. The economics depend on whether the hardware, and above all the engines, can be turned around and flown again without costly rework. Passing acceptance tests after a real flight and landing is the first evidence on that point for a Chinese commercial vehicle.

The stage in question came down on August 19, when the Y2 flight returned its first stage to a landing pad at Minqin County in Gansu province. The report describes that as planned, and it follows the December 2025 debut flight, in which the second stage reached orbit but the booster failed to land softly.

LandSpace has designed the first stage for at least 20 flights, according to the report. Zhang tied cheaper launch to a larger space economy, with denser satellite constellations and faster-refreshing remote sensing data.

## What isn't known

The report gives no thrust figures, burn durations or inspection findings, and it does not say whether or when the recovered stage, or just its engines, will fly again. Nor has the claim been independently verified; it rests on a single executive's remarks relayed by Chinese media. A reflight date would be the next thing to watch for, since no Chinese company has yet flown a recovered orbital-class booster a second time.
