Asteroid-mining startup Karman+ has published a company update arguing that its product is fuel, not metal. In a post dated November 23, the company says it plans to sell propellant in space, starting with water extracted from asteroids, which sidesteps the cost of lifting it from Earth.

The processing path it describes runs from regolith clay to water, then to oxygen and hydrogen, and with added carbon to oxygen and methane. Karman+ says the same clays also hold carbon and nitrogen compounds, which could support a wider range of fuels later. Its targets are C-type asteroids, which it says contain roughly 2 to 10 percent water by mass, locked in hydrated minerals and clay rather than surface ice.

## Why target supply now

The company points to the Vera Rubin Observatory's survey, which began earlier this year, as a source of targets. Karman+ says early data suggests more than 100,000 new asteroid discoveries this year. By its estimate, about 10 percent are near-Earth and about half of those are C-type, which works out to roughly 5,000 candidates a year, close to 100 a week.

The demand case rests on orbital refueling, which NASA's Artemis architecture needs and which has not been demonstrated yet. Karman+ notes that NASA's updated plan adds a second refueling step, a Final Tanking Orbit, after low Earth orbit. It calculates that a full Starship lunar refill would take about 15 fuel launches, and that at the launch rates it assumes for 2028 and 2029 this would take weeks to months. It cites Starship carrying about 50 tons to low Earth orbit on Flight 15 and puts current launch cost at about $100 million, excluding development. The company itself acknowledges that boil-off, transfer losses and launch coordination are unresolved.

The mismatch it highlights is scale: its current refueling customers need 500 to 5,000 kilograms of propellant, against roughly 1,500,000 kilograms for a full Starship refill.

## Spacecraft changes

The update also lists design changes to the company's spacecraft:

- **Propulsion:** one larger Hall effect thruster replaces three small ones, the reaction control system gets more fuel margin, and the vehicle can be refueled in orbit.
- **Surface probes:** sensor-laden darts, which the company calls space torpedoes, would be released from about 100 meters up to measure thermal, inertial and subsurface properties over several hours, helping pick excavation sites.
- **Communications:** an X-band system with a Johns Hopkins APL-heritage radio and a 110 W amplifier, designed for more than 1 Mbps from about 50 million kilometers using a commercial ground station, since NASA's Deep Space Network is not available to it.
- **Power:** mission changes introduced a "super eclipse" risk three to four times longer than a low Earth orbit eclipse. Avoiding that trajectory would cost one to two weeks.

Karman+ also reports presenting its Apophis work and asteroid water roadmap at a NASA Ames and USGS offworld resources conference, where AstroForge's Matt Gialich and Transastra's Joel Sercel also attended. All figures here are the company's own claims and estimates; none have been independently verified.
