The US Federal Communications Commission plans to vote in December on opening a rulemaking that would make foreign-licensed satellite operators face in the United States the same kinds of restrictions their home countries place on American providers. According to The Register, FCC Chairman Brendan Carr said the proposal would apply "those or similar regulatory restraints" to operators from those countries. The stated aim is to "ensure reciprocity and fair dealing."

## What is behind it

The move follows a consultation the FCC opened in March. Carr argues that US providers face "regulatory and spectrum limitations" that foreign competitors do not, and that this "regulatory asymmetry" has to end.

The trigger is mainly Europe. The FCC points to the EU's proposed Digital Networks Act, which would harmonise connectivity rules across the bloc. Under it, EU-wide authorisation of satellite spectrum could replace national licensing and force US systems to be relicensed in Europe. A separate proposal would restructure authorisation of mobile satellite service in the 2 GHz band after existing rights expire in May 2027. CCS Insight analyst Joe Gardiner is quoted as saying the EU would reserve roughly two-thirds of that spectrum for EU-based, EU-owned operators, effectively shutting out SpaceX and Amazon Leo from spectrum valuable for direct-to-device services.

## The UK is in the frame too

Britain is outside the EU, but the FCC cites the UK Space Agency spending about 75 percent of its 2023/24 budget on European Space Agency procurements that US firms cannot bid for. British satellite operators could therefore face limits on US market access if the rules are adopted.

## What to watch

Reporting differs on the scope of the December vote: some outlets describe it as a vote on restrictions, others as a vote on whether to begin a rulemaking to reconsider how foreign-licensed systems get market access. A rulemaking vote would start a comment process, not impose rules immediately. SpaceWatch.Global reports that roughly a quarter of the more than 200 satellite systems approved under the FCC's Part 25 rules are licensed by foreign administrations. No formal European response had been reported at the time of writing.
