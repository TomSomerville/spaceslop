Oregon and Virginia have become the first US states to sue SpaceX over Grok, the AI image generator that sits inside X. The legal risk of SpaceX's AI business now lands on a company better known for rockets.

Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield submitted his complaint on Sunday evening to the Multnomah County Circuit Court. Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones followed on Monday morning in the Circuit Court for the City of Richmond. According to CNBC, neither court had formally accepted its filing as of early Monday. The states are acting separately rather than in a joint case.

## What the states allege

Both complaints say SpaceX failed to put safeguards in place to stop Grok and X users from creating and sharing harmful deepfakes, including alleged AI-generated child sexual abuse material. They also say the company falsely marketed Grok and X as generally safe. The claims rest on state consumer-protection law: Virginia's Consumer Protection Act and Oregon's Unlawful Trade Practices Act. CNBC notes that X allows users from age 13.

Both states want injunctions, civil penalties, disgorgement of profits and legal costs. Virginia also seeks restitution for consumers, and Oregon is asking for stricter guardrails on SpaceX's products.

## Why it matters to a space company

xAI is now part of SpaceX, which is why the rocket maker is the named defendant. Press coverage describes the two suits as part of a wider pile: SpaceX also faces a number of lawsuits from individuals over Grok-generated images, and Baltimore has sued xAI separately. Virginia had flagged this possibility earlier; MLex reported in October that SpaceX disclosed in a securities filing that the Virginia attorney general was contemplating a suit over its statements about Grok.

SpaceX did not immediately respond to CNBC's request for comment. In the Baltimore case, CNBC reports, the company has argued that Section 230 shields it from liability. In a federal case in Texas, its lawyers called Grok "a neutral tool, subject to user control." At an October New York City Council hearing, a SpaceX AI policy lead said the company has "safeguards" and "strict controls" for its AI products.

None of this has been tested in court yet, and the allegations are the states' own. For investors and customers watching SpaceX as a launch and satellite business, the practical question is how much legal and regulatory cost the AI side adds, and whether state attorneys general beyond these two follow.
