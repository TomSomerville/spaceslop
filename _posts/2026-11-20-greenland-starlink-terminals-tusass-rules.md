Greenland's government has confirmed that using Starlink outside the state-owned operator Tusass is against the law in the territory, while signalling that it has little appetite to police it. The statement came in reply to a parliamentary question alleging that Danish soldiers stationed there use privately owned terminals.

## What was asked and answered

On 23 October, Greenlandic politician Pele Broberg filed a question claiming, citing multiple sources, that Danish soldiers bring personal Starlink terminals to Greenland and use them during their postings. His argument was one of fairness: if soldiers can connect through Starlink, Greenlanders should be able to as well.

The government's answer, published on 5 November, said it had no confirmed information about soldiers using private terminals. It did acknowledge reports of Starlink terminals installed at various places in Greenland. Under the Telecommunications Act, Starlink use outside Tusass is prohibited, and the law carries no exemption for private individuals.

The government also said enforcement resources are limited. Cases where a terminal does not interfere with other lawful radio communications would generally be a lower priority, according to the reply.

## Why it matters

The episode is a small example of a recurring friction in the satellite broadband business: a constellation that works anywhere on Earth runs into national licensing regimes built around a single domestic carrier. Greenland's rules protect Tusass, and the government stressed the importance of universal service obligations, which tie the operator to serving remote communities.

Broberg is pursuing a broader goal of letting Greenlanders buy satellite internet directly from providers rather than through Tusass. He started a parliamentary debate on the subject on 20 October.

The result for now is an awkward gap between written law and practice. Terminals are reportedly in use, the ban stands, and the government has said it will not prioritise cases that cause no interference. Whether that tolerance holds, or the rules change, will depend on the debate Broberg has opened. The reporting rests on a single outlet's account of the parliamentary exchange, and the claim about soldiers remains unconfirmed.
