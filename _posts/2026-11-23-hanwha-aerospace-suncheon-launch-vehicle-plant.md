Hanwha Aerospace has finished building a launch-vehicle production site in Suncheon, South Korea, which it calls the country's largest private facility of its kind. The completion ceremony was held on November 4 at the Yulchon Industrial Complex in Jeonnam province, with about 300 attendees, according to the Korean outlet Newstomato. Korean coverage resurfaced this week as Hanwha's launcher ambitions drew fresh attention.

The site covers roughly 18,000 pyeong, about 59,500 square meters. Hanwha is the lead integrator for the Nuri rocket, and the plant will handle assembly and performance testing of Nuri's first to third stages, plus the 75-ton engine for the sixth Nuri launch planned for next year. It is also meant to build the next-generation launch vehicle, reusable-technology demonstrators, and eventually Hanwha's own rocket. The company says it will later add production of methane engines and propellant tanks.

## The timeline

Hanwha's stated sequence is to finish current government programs, including the Nuri upgrade work, then enter the global space transport market with its own launch vehicle in 2029, and begin commercial launch services for Nuri and the next-generation vehicle in 2033. One Korean outlet's headline (UpKorea) frames the same plan as a first flight of the company's own rocket in 2028; the sources I read describe 2029 as the market-entry year, so the two dates may refer to a first flight and commercial entry, but that is my inference rather than something either report states.

## The money

Hanwha Group plans to invest 55 trillion won in space, aviation and AI by 2040, of which Hanwha Aerospace expects about 23 trillion won to go into space transport. The company also announced a shared-growth program with 40 partner firms across Nuri, the next-generation vehicle and its own rocket.

Kim Sun, who heads Hanwha Aerospace's space business, called the plant "a core infrastructure for Korea's leap into a privately led New Space era" (translated from Korean).

## Why it matters

South Korea has relied on the state-run Korea Aerospace Research Institute for launch vehicle design, with industry building to government specification. Hanwha is positioning itself to own a vehicle outright and sell launches commercially, a shift that mirrors what happened in the US a decade earlier. Production capacity is the easy part to announce; a flight-proven vehicle and a manifest of paying customers are what the 2029 and 2033 targets will actually hinge on.
