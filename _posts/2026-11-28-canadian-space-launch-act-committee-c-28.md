Canada's first dedicated rocket-launch law moved a step closer on Thursday. The House of Commons voted 196-130 to pass Bill C-28, the Canadian Space Launch Act, at second reading, sending it to the Standing Committee on Transport, Infrastructure and Communities. According to SpaceQ, the committee held its first meeting on the bill the same day, before the formal referral, and the witnesses mostly argued the bill is needed but incomplete.

The bill amends the Aeronautics Act so the transport minister can issue permits for launches and re-entries, certify launch sites, and indemnify operators against third-party claims beyond what their insurance covers. Until now, Transport Canada has handled applications case by case under interim rules in place since 2023.

## What witnesses wanted changed

NordSpace's launch program director, Volodymyr Prystaiko, said the bill does not explicitly cover ground testing, which today falls under "a patchwork of municipal, provincial and some federal laws." The company's brief proposes five amendments, including a definition of "rocket test activity" and risk-based rules. He also stressed that indemnity matters for early launches, before operators can buy commercial cover. NordSpace says its Taiga test-flight application has a "conditional go" from Transport Canada.

Adam Trumpour of the Launch Canada Rocketry Association asked MPs to keep student and hobby rockets out of the orbital-launch regime: "A hobby or an amateur rocket should not be treated as if it were an orbital launch vehicle."

Andrea Harrington of McGill's Institute of Air and Space Law called the bill "a welcome and overdue step" but listed gaps. It names the Liability Convention but not the Outer Space Treaty, whose Article 6 requires states to authorize and supervise private space activity. It does not amend the law governing the Transportation Safety Board, so a re-entry accident could lack a clear investigator. It covers only launches from and re-entries into Canada, not Canadian companies launching abroad. And it says little on the environment or Indigenous consultation. "The risk is that the bill places too few requirements on the regulator," she said.

Salomé Paradis of Université Laval and Université Toulouse Capitole noted that key terms, including "launch" itself, are left to regulation, though the start and end of a launch determine operator liability. She urged mandatory insurance or another financial guarantee.

Space Canada CEO Brian Gallant said the Canadian Space Agency's mandate has nothing to do with national security. Conservative MP Dan Albas pressed that point, saying the bill has no national-security screening of who launches or what flies, and is "completely silent on indemnification," leaving it to ministerial discretion.

## Why it matters

Canada has launch ambitions, including Spaceport Nova Scotia, but no statute to license them. Industry Minister Mélanie Joly also opened consultations in October on a separate Canadian space act, with submissions due December 21. Committee amendments will show how far MPs are willing to go on liability and security before the bill returns to the House.
