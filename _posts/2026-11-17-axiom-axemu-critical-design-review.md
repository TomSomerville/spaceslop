Axiom Space has closed the Critical Design Review for its Axiom Extravehicular Mobility Unit, the AxEMU, the spacesuit NASA is buying to replace its decades-old ISS suits and to put boots back on the Moon. The company announced the milestone on 13 November, calling it a major step toward NASA certifying the suit for flight.

## What CDR actually means here

A critical design review is the point where a program stops asking "will this design work?" and starts asking "can we build and test it?" According to Axiom, every component team had to present finished analysis backed by prototypes that had been built and tested, covering component packages, engineering drawings and structural analyses. The review also looked at the whole suit as a system, including integrated mass properties and pressure-drop performance, which the earlier preliminary design review didn't require.

Clearing it, the company says, gives increased assurance that its design and fabrication plans are ready for production and qualification. "With the closure of CDR, we've delivered the large body of work that provides Axiom Space confidence going into production," said Tammy Radford, program manager for Axiom's Extravehicular Activity Services (xEVAS) work.

## What comes next

Next comes qualification testing: performance runs, environmental testing such as thermal vacuum and vibration, and life-cycle testing. In parallel, Axiom is assembling the full suit, including the pressure garment and the Portable Life Support System, with a target of finishing by early 2027. After qualification and certification tests, the suit is to be delivered for launch to the International Space Station for a demonstration. Axiom gave no date for that flight.

The work has moved quickly by spacesuit standards. Axiom says it began this phase in summer 2025 and delivered its first suit components and flight support equipment to NASA before the end of that year.

The ISS suit is only half the job. Axiom is also developing a Sortie suit, the AxEMU-S1, a configuration tailored to short lunar surface excursions on early Artemis missions. The release ties that to NASA returning astronauts to the Moon in 2028. That is the agency's stated target, and the suit still has qualification and certification ahead of it.

Everything here comes from Axiom's own announcement. NASA has not yet published its own account of the review.
