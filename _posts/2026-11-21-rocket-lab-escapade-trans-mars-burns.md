Rocket Lab says the two ESCAPADE spacecraft it built for NASA have completed their trans-Mars injection burns, the maneuvers that put them on course for the Red Planet. The probes, named Blue and Gold, fired on Nov. 7 and Nov. 9, 2026, two days apart.

Each burn was a single uninterrupted engine firing of about 10 minutes, performed during a powered Earth flyby. Rocket Lab said the spacecraft ran the maneuvers autonomously while its own team and operators from UC Berkeley's Space Sciences Laboratory watched. The company reported overall accuracy well above 99 percent and a final attitude error of less than 1 degree.

## Why this one is unusual

Interplanetary probes normally get their departure push from the launch vehicle's upper stage. ESCAPADE did not. The twins launched on Nov. 13, 2025, from Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, then spent nearly a year in a loiter orbit around the Sun-Earth Lagrange point 2 before swinging back past Earth. The burns therefore had to come from the spacecraft's own propulsion system, which needed enough thrust to do the job and a carefully planned trajectory to make the Earth gravity assist pay off.

Rocket Lab describes the injection burn as one of the mission's two biggest propulsive events, the other being Mars orbit insertion, and notes it could not be split into smaller segments. "Performing these TMI maneuvers flawlessly is a testament to the relentless execution of the Rocket Lab team," CEO Sir Peter Beck said.

## What's next

Mars orbit insertion is scheduled for September 2027, with the first science formation expected in 2028. The mission is Rocket Lab's first interplanetary spacecraft build, and it lands in the middle of a competition over NASA's Mars Telecommunications Orbiter, which went to Blue Origin and which Rocket Lab is challenging. Spacecraft that arrive at Mars on schedule are a useful exhibit for a company making the case that it can build deep-space hardware.
