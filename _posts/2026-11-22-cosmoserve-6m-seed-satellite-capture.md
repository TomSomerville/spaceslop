Cosmoserve Space, a Hyderabad startup working on satellite servicing and debris removal, announced a $6 million seed round on November 5, led by Japan's Rebright Partners. Aranya Holdings and Naandi Ventures joined as new backers, while existing investors AUM Ventures and Shakti VC increased their stakes. Total funding now exceeds $9 million, including a $3.17 million pre-seed from September 2025.

## A soft grip for tumbling targets

The company's core technology is a soft robotic capture system, modelled on a Venus flytrap, designed to grip tumbling objects that have no docking fixture. Each mission would use a two-spacecraft unit that can refuel satellites and clear several pieces of debris per trip. Cosmoserve estimates that this approach could cut the cost of servicing a single satellite from roughly $10 million to under $1 million, though that is the company's own figure and no customer has yet tested it.

Founder and CEO Chiranjeevi Phanindra set up the company in June 2025 after about 15 years at ISRO, where he was deputy project director on the Gaganyaan crewed programme. "Primarily, the core technology we are building is a soft robotic capture system," he said.

## One failed demo, more on the way

The first in-orbit demonstrator, Embrace-1, flew on Skyroot's Vikram-1 rocket, but its robotic petals did not deploy. The company says the fix targets the thermal release mechanism and low-temperature effects. A revised capture system, Embrace-2, is due to fly in the coming months, with another demonstration planned for next year. All demos are meant to be finished in 2027, with commercial operations starting in 2028. A later full-scale demonstration aims to approach within 10 metres of a dead satellite.

Cosmoserve has letters of intent from four satellite operators but no paying customers. It has built a Hyderabad lab, Base 1, at a cost of about Rs 6 crore, and says about 60 percent of mission parts are made in India.

The pitch lands in a crowded field. Servicing and debris-removal startups in the US, Europe and Japan are chasing the same operators, and a flight failure on the first demo is a handicap the company will need to erase quickly. Still, a seed round of this size gives Cosmoserve the runway for at least one more attempt.
