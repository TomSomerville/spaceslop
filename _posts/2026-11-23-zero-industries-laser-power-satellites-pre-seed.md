Zero Industries, a San Francisco-based public benefit corporation with a Japanese subsidiary, has raised a $3 million (about 470 million yen) pre-seed round to build a service that beams power by laser from one satellite to another. The round was announced on October 22 and led by Maezawa Fund, with UntroD Capital Japan, NOW, Midtown and individual investors taking part.

The founding team is unusually young. The Bridge reports that CEO Umi Sugihara is 16, CTO Yusei Fujii is 19 and COO Koya Tani is 20. The company is roughly six months old.

## What Helios would do

The first product, Helios, has three parts: a transmitter satellite called Helios Beam that generates power in space and sends it by laser, a receiver module called Helios Cell that customer satellites would carry, and a software system, Helios Grid, to manage the transfers. The design aims at distances of hundreds of kilometers and at feeding several satellites from one unit.

The pitch is that satellites normally carry their own solar arrays and batteries to ride out eclipses in Earth's shadow. If power can be delivered from outside, that mass could go to instruments, computing or propulsion instead. Early targets named by the company are Earth observation satellites, orbital transfer vehicles and inspection or repair craft. Space data centers and lunar bases are listed as later customers.

## Roadmap

Zero Industries is working with the lab of Kazuhisa Fujita at the Graduate School of Optical Science Creation in Hamamatsu on wireless laser power transmission for satellites. From autumn 2026 to spring 2027 the plan is to evaluate receiver components and run outdoor transmission tests over distances of about a kilometer. The company's target for a first satellite-to-satellite power transfer demonstration is 2030, after which it would start supplying power from commercial transmission satellites.

## Why it matters, and the caveats

Power delivery between spacecraft is a recurring idea in the in-space servicing and orbital compute pitches, and a number of startups are chasing space-based power beaming. The gap here is large: the company is at component evaluation, with a flight demonstration roughly four years out, and $3 million is a small sum for a spacecraft program. The report is based on the company's press release and contains no independent technical assessment or quotes from investors, so the performance claims remain unverified.
