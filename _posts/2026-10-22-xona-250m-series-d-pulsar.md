Xona Space Systems, the Burlingame, California company building a commercial low Earth orbit navigation network called Pulsar, announced a $250 million Series D at a $2.45 billion valuation on October 22. Total capital raised now exceeds $570 million. Future Ventures, led by Steve Jurvetson, led the round, with new and existing investors participating. Xona describes Jurvetson, a longtime SpaceX board member, as an early backer.

The money is earmarked for manufacturing capacity, more satellites, the systems needed to run Pulsar as a global service, and continued improvement of the technology after launch.

## Six satellites, two kinds

The announcement coincided with the launch of six Pulsar satellites that week. According to Xona, four will start serving customers in 2027. The other two are test satellites, built entirely in-house at Xona's Burlingame factory, which the company says is set up to turn out several spacecraft per week. In a separate post, Xona said the four service satellites use a modified bus from Pulsar-0, its earlier demonstrator built with Aerospacelab, and that the six were planned for a Bandwagon-5 rideshare launch. That post carries inconsistent dates, so treat the exact launch timing with some care.

Initial service would cover the U.S., Canada and most of Europe, with eight broadcasts a day of about 25 minutes each, according to Xona. Revisit times of roughly 12 hours are expected with the first batch, which is why this is a start of a production constellation rather than a working navigation service.

## Why it matters

Pulsar aims to complement GPS and other GNSS signals from a much lower orbit, where signals arrive stronger. Xona reports that Pulsar-0 completed more than 500 transmissions, delivered positioning better than two centimeters, and outperformed traditional GNSS in difficult environments including jamming and spoofing. The company also says existing commercial receivers can use the signal after an upgrade, with no hardware changes. These are company claims from its own tests, and independent verification is not part of the announcement.

Xona has also received what it calls first-of-its-kind FCC authorization for a commercial constellation of this type. Its earlier announcements included a new operations chief as Pulsar moves toward scaled production.

CEO Brian Manning called Pulsar "the first step" toward the company's larger ambition. The real test comes in 2027, when four satellites need to prove a commercial service that customers will pay for.
