NASA has asked the commercial space industry what it could do about the Hubble Space Telescope, which is slowly sinking toward an uncontrolled reentry. The agency posted a request for information (RFI) at the end of October covering three possible missions: raising Hubble's orbit, upgrading it during a servicing visit, or grappling it for a controlled reentry and disposal. Responses are due November 30.

## What NASA is asking for

An RFI is not a request for proposals, and it carries no commitment to fund anything. The Register notes that the notice gives no indication of expected cost. What it does set out is a timeline and a few ground rules. NASA wants any mission flown by 2032, and it must not harm the telescope. One option is a boost to at least 600 km, according to the Register's reading of the notice. Another is an upgrade that improves Hubble's performance, resilience and expected lifetime on orbit.

The clock is the reason. The Register reports that NASA estimates a 10 percent chance Hubble reenters by September 2032 and a 50 percent chance by December 2033. Hubble has no propulsion of its own, so without intervention the reentry would be uncontrolled, and the telescope is massive enough that the article describes a remote chance of harm on the ground. Higher solar activity can thicken the upper atmosphere and speed the decay beyond predictions, as happened with the Swift observatory.

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said Hubble "still has important science ahead of it," according to ExecutiveGov, and argued that finding cost-effective ways to extend the life of assets already in orbit serves taxpayers and the science community. Science Mission Directorate head Nicky Fox said NASA wants to explore a wide range of ways to lift Hubble while it develops its planned follow-on flagship mission.

## Why industry should care

This is a rare government-defined market for servicing a large, high-value asset that was never designed to be refuelled or repaired by robots. The Register points out that Hubble carries a soft-capture mechanism fitted during the last shuttle servicing mission, STS-125 in May 2009, which should make docking easier than the grappling NASA had planned for Swift. Northrop Grumman's Mission Extension Vehicle is the usual reference point for life-extension servicing, though the Register describes Hubble as an order of magnitude harder.

There is also history here. The Register notes that Isaacman advocated a SpaceX mission to boost Hubble in 2022, before he led NASA, and that the idea was rejected. With him now running the agency, the article suggests similar concepts could be revisited. The RFI answers should show which companies, if any, think they can do it by 2032.

The other pressure is the lack of a replacement. The Register says nothing comparable is expected before the 2040s, when the Habitable Worlds Observatory could come online, while Hubble time remains oversubscribed seven to one despite JWST and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope. Hubble recently moved from three-gyro to one-gyro operation to extend its working life.

ExecutiveGov also notes that NASA recently issued a final request for proposals for commercial space stations, with proposals due December 8 and awards expected April 15, 2027.
