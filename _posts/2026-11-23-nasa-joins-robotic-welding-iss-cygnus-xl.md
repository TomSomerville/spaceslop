NASA has published details of JOINS, short for JOining Demonstrations IN Space, a technology demonstration that aims to show robots welding metal in the exposed environment of space. Lockheed Martin leads the project under a NASA Tipping Point award, managed through the agency's Research and Technology Mission Directorate.

The payload is due to ride to the International Space Station on Northrop Grumman's CRS-25 mission aboard a Cygnus XL cargo spacecraft, with an estimated launch in November 2026. The welding demonstration itself is estimated for March 2027, according to NASA's page, which was last updated on November 22.

## What will be tested

The experiment will run inside the station's Bishop Airlock and will weld two metals, aluminum and stainless steel, using two different methods. Laser beam welding uses a precise beam to melt and fuse material, which suits small or thin parts. Friction stir welding uses a rotating tool to soften and stir metals together without melting them, an approach NASA says can reduce cracking and warping.

Cameras on the payload will send data to Earth during the work. The welded test articles will come back to Earth months later for hands-on inspection, which is where the real verdict on joint quality will be delivered.

## Why it matters

The pitch is simple: anything launched is limited by the size of the rocket fairing, and welding in orbit would let companies and agencies assemble structures larger than any vehicle can carry. NASA lists large antennas, solar arrays, radiators, sunshades and optical systems as candidates, with an eye on future Moon and Mars missions.

Orbital assembly has become a recurring theme for startups as well. SpaceSlop covered yesterday [Space Quarters' seed round for orbital welding](/2026/11/22/space-quarters-5m-seed-orbital-welding/), so the technology is drawing both government and venture money. NASA's page describes the goal in a single line: to "demonstrate robotic welding in exposed space."

The schedule is an estimate on both ends, and the timeline depends on the Cygnus XL launch happening when planned. NASA's page did not give further detail on the specific launch date or on the payload's mass.
