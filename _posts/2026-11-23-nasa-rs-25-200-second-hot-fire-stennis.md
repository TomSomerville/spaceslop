NASA fired a developmental RS-25 engine for its full planned 200 seconds, or 3 minutes 20 seconds, at Stennis Space Center in Mississippi on Nov. 18, according to a report on the test. The engine, No. 10001, is a development unit that will not fly.

The firing took place on the Fred Haise Test Stand near Bay St. Louis. NASA and prime contractor L3Harris Technologies ran the test, with operators from NASA, L3Harris and Nova Space Solutions. NASA says the aim is to "capture critical data to improve engine performance and reliability for future Artemis missions." The report does not say which data was collected or why a shorter duration was chosen.

## Follow-up to October

The same engine ran for about 500 seconds on Oct. 21, matching the burn profile of an SLS launch, and tested a new nozzle thermal protection system. Longer firings simulate a full ascent; this one is a shorter run on the same hardware.

## Where RS-25 stands

Every RS-25 is tested and certified flightworthy at Stennis before use. Each Artemis mission uses four of them, producing up to 2 million pounds of thrust together. The first four Artemis missions use upgraded engines left over from the space shuttle program. The first new-build flight engine, No. 20001, fired for 500 seconds at up to 111 percent power in June 2025 and is slated for the fifth SLS launch. NASA awarded L3Harris a 2015 contract to restart RS-25 design and production.

The report notes NASA plans an Artemis IV lunar landing in 2028, with an SLS launching astronauts aboard Orion from Kennedy Space Center to support exploration of the lunar south pole.
