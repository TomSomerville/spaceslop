China put the 29th group of its low-Earth-orbit internet satellites into orbit on a Long March 12 rocket, lifting off from the Hainan commercial space launch site in Wenchang at 4:24 a.m. on Monday 9 November, according to state broadcaster CGTN. That corresponds to 20:24 UTC on 8 November in public launch trackers. CGTN reported that the satellites reached their preset orbit.

CGTN did not say how many satellites were aboard. Chinese state coverage of the Guowang constellation, run by China Satellite Network Group, routinely leaves the count and the orbital parameters out of the initial announcement.

## The rocket

The Long March 12 is about 62 meters long and burns cryogenic liquid propellants, which makes it part of China's newer, cleaner-burning generation of launchers. CGTN describes it as the country's first single-core rocket in the 4-meter-class diameter range, able to lift at least 6 tonnes to a 700-kilometer sun-synchronous orbit and built to deploy either single or multiple satellites into different orbits. The broadcaster counted the mission as the 676th flight of the Long March series.

## Why it matters

Group 29 came about two weeks before group 30, which flew on a Long March 8A from the same Hainan complex on 20 November (see [our earlier report](/2026/11/20/guowang-group-30-long-march-8a/)). Back-to-back launches from the Wenchang commercial pads show how China is using several vehicle types in parallel to build out its answer to Starlink and Amazon Leo, rather than relying on a single workhorse rocket.

The caveat is how little the source discloses. This article rests on a single outlet's short report, so details such as satellite count, booster fate and the exact orbit are unconfirmed here. Independent trackers list the launch as successful, in line with CGTN's account.
