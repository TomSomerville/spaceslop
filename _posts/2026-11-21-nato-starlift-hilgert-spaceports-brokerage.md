A NATO official has spelled out how the alliance sees its new Starlift initiative: as a way to organize military launch demand, not a way to add rockets. Daniel Hilgert, head of section for Space Defence Industry in NATO's Innovation and Armaments division, laid out the long-term goals in an interview with Via Satellite from Izmir, Turkey, where NATO has been holding its Edge defense technology event. The initiative itself was covered in [our earlier article](/2026/11/19/nato-starlift-launch-initiative/); the interview adds detail on where it is headed.

## A brokerage, with limits

Hilgert was direct about what Starlift cannot do. "It cannot create additional rockets overnight, but it can help organize demand and give launch providers a clearer basis for investment," he said. Launch access, he said, is a real constraint for both defense users and industry, and the alliance needs "more launch capacity and reliable access for companies of all sizes."

Via Satellite reports that the aim is a working launch brokerage, with coordinated military requirements and tested arrangements for more responsive space operations. Nations will set the timeline for initial operating capability. Ten allies fund the effort through the NATO Support and Procurement Agency, with South Korea also committed.

Hilgert's yardstick is practical: success means participating countries can arrange suitable launches more effectively, and have more reliable options when circumstances change.

## Spaceports and rideshare

The more consequential comments concern infrastructure. NATO has historically paid for military airfields, fuel pipelines and communications facilities, and Hilgert said a similar approach could extend to spaceports if allies agree it is needed. The EU already plays a major financing role in Europe's spaceports, and NATO could contribute through its Security Investment Programme, though any investment would stay subject to the program's eligibility criteria and allied approval. No spaceport has been named for funding.

On rideshare, he called it one possible option within the brokerage: cost-effective for planned deployments, while time-sensitive missions may need other arrangements.

He framed the network as distributed, drawing on capabilities in Europe, Canada and partners such as South Korea. "Resilience means having more than one route to orbit, in particular in a crisis or conflict," he said, adding that launch sites need protection and the ability to keep operating through disruption, with security attention comparable to military airbases. Allies will also work with industry on standards where helpful.

## Why it matters

For European launch startups and spaceport operators, the possibility of NATO infrastructure money is the interesting part, even though it is conditional and unspecified. Starlift still has not named launch providers or published budgets, so whether it ends up steering business to European small launchers, American providers or both remains open.
