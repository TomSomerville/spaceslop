A Caltech robot in low Earth orbit has autonomously assembled part of an antenna frame, which the team describes as the first structure built in space without astronaut help. Project lead Sergio Pellegrino said: "This is the first time that a structure has been autonomously constructed in space."

The system, ASTRA (Autonomous In-Space Tensioned Reflector Assembly), started with two truss bays built on the ground and then assembled a third on its own from composite tubes, aluminum connectors and dispensers. The target was a 1.4-meter reflector antenna with a perforated reflective film, co-invented with the University of Pennsylvania. The full design needs 11 bays, and the team does not expect to complete them before the mission ends in December.

## How it got there

ASTRA is funded by DARPA's NOM4D program (Novel Orbital and Moon Manufacturing, Materials, and Mass-efficient Design), which began in 2022; it is one of two projects in the program's in-orbit phase. It launched on March 30, 2026 on SpaceX's Transporter-16 rideshare from Vandenberg, aboard Momentus's Vigoride 7 vehicle, which carried 10 payloads. The payload takes up about a cubic meter. The team had under 12 months to build, test and deliver the hardware.

Operations were hands-on despite the autonomy: a three-person ground team sent commands during seven to eight daily contact windows of about 15 minutes each, using a flight-spare unit at Caltech to reproduce problems before sending fixes.

## Why it matters

Technical difficulties and scheduling pressure kept ASTRA from finishing, and the team reports only an initial section built. Still, it is evidence that robotic assembly works in orbit. The pitch is that structures could grow beyond the size limits of a rocket fairing: large radio antennas, giant telescopes and space solar power arrays. DARPA's Andrew Detor said that "Nothing approaching that scale can be achieved today."

The in-space manufacturing field has plenty of ideas and few orbital demonstrations, so even a partial result counts. The report does not cover how a finished structure would hold its precise shape through years of thermal cycling.
