CesiumAstro, the Austin-based maker of phased-array antennas and spacecraft, announced a $730 million Series D round on November 5 at a valuation above $4.5 billion. The money is meant to turn a company that sells communications hardware into one that owns and operates its own satellite network.

Janus Henderson led the round. Insight Partners, Sands Capital, Sumitomo Corporation of Americas, Toyota's Woven Capital and Magnetar are among the participants. CesiumAstro also lined up $100 million in debt from the Export-Import Bank of the United States and JPMorgan. Outlets differ on whether that debt sits inside or on top of the $730 million, so treat the headline figure with care.

## Synchronicity

The network is called Synchronicity: a planned 737-satellite constellation aimed at commercial, sovereign and defense customers. According to Via Satellite it uses a dynamically managed multi-beam design across Ku-, Ka- and V-band, with onboard edge compute and multi-orbit operation. CesiumAstro filed with the FCC for the system in July. No deployment schedule was given in the announcement.

"Synchronicity is the culmination of the technology stack we have methodically built since CesiumAstro was founded," CEO Shey Sabripour said.

The company's first satellite, Element-1, flew on SpaceX's Transporter rideshare mission in October and has begun operating in orbit.

## Why it matters

CesiumAstro began as a phased-array technology provider, then moved into ground antennas and full satellites, and has bought Jariet Technologies, 1Aardvark and Vidrovr to add semiconductor, mission-software and AI capability. Funds go to technology development, US manufacturing and spacecraft production, international expansion and the constellation itself. Dealroom reports a $500 million, five-year buildout of headquarters and manufacturing space in the Austin area, though that detail comes from a single source.

The round follows a February raise this year; sources disagree on its size. The move puts CesiumAstro in a crowded field of well-funded constellation builders, and sets up a bet that governments want constellations they control rather than service from a large incumbent network.
