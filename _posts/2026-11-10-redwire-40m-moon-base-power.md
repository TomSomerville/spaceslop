Redwire has been selected for an award of about $40 million to supply an integrated power generation system for NASA's Moon Base effort, the company announced on 10 November. The system takes the company's Roll-Out Solar Array, or ROSA, as its core and is meant as a pathfinder for the power that future lunar rovers, habitats and in-situ resource utilization (making use of local materials such as ice) will need.

## What NASA is testing

According to Redwire, NASA's Moon Base Infrastructure Office will oversee an autonomous, integrated power demonstration near the lunar South Pole. The agency plans to buy commercial components for what the release calls a Mini-Communications and Solar Tower, or miniCAST, including solar arrays, batteries and wireless power-transfer hardware.

The demonstration is built to answer three practical questions: whether solar arrays can be deployed vertically on a mast, whether payloads can operate from that mast, and whether the system survives the lunar night. The last one is the hard part. Near the pole the Sun sits low on the horizon, which is why vertical arrays are attractive, but a long dark period still means batteries and thermal design carry the load.

## Heritage

Redwire says it previously adapted ROSA for vertical deployment and retraction in lunar gravity under NASA's Vertical Solar Array Technology program. ROSA was developed through a NASA public-private partnership between Redwire and Boeing, and the company says it has delivered or is developing more than 65 solar array wings.

The announcement comes days after the company said the eighth and final Redwire-built ROSA wing was installed on the International Space Station, completing a power upgrade there. Mike Gold, president of Redwire Space, called Moon Base "one of the most important human spaceflight initiatives for America since Apollo."

The release doesn't give a schedule, a launch vehicle or a landing site, and it describes the award as a selection rather than a signed final contract value, so those details remain to be seen. For now it is a modest but concrete data point on how NASA intends to buy lunar surface infrastructure: in demonstration-sized pieces from commercial suppliers.
