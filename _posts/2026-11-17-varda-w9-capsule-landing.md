Varda Space Industries has recovered its W-9 capsule after a landing at the Koonibba Test Range in South Australia, the company announced on 17 November. According to Varda, W-9 touched down at 5:56 a.m. Pacific time after 48 days in orbit.

The landing completes what Varda calls a double homecoming. W-9 and W-8 launched together on 1 October 2026, which Varda describes as the first time two commercial reentry vehicles have flown on the same rocket. W-8 came home first, landing on 5 November at 5:58 a.m. Pacific time. That makes W-9 the company's second reentry in the same month.

## What W-9 was carrying

Varda says W-9 flew a government-funded system that captures data during hypersonic flight, the regime where a capsule is moving fast enough that the air around it becomes a plasma-laced thermal problem. The nose tile also carried small samples of next-generation composites being developed for ablative thermal protection, meaning heat shield material designed to char and erode in a controlled way. Those samples, the company says, took the full heat of reentry, so their condition after landing is the real test result.

Varda hasn't published any findings from the hardware yet. That matters because reentry data is a large part of the business case: the company positions itself both as an in-orbit pharmaceutical processing outfit and as a provider of hypersonic test opportunities.

## Three vehicles at once

W-7 is still in orbit. Varda says it is healthy, expects to share mission updates in the coming weeks, and plans to bring it back before the end of the year. An earlier Varda release, listed on the company's media page, said W-7 launched on 20 October to study cancer drug crystallization in orbit. For a stretch this month, then, Varda had three vehicles operating in space at once, run by its mission operations team in El Segundo, California.

Wendy Shimata, Varda's VP of Autonomous Systems, said in the release that "every successful return proves that regular, reliable access to orbit and back is increasingly routine." That is the company's pitch in a sentence, and the cadence backs it up: three launches' worth of capsules in roughly six weeks, two of them recovered in under two. Whether the economics hold depends on customers paying for the science and test data, which this release doesn't address.
