United Launch Alliance has narrowed the cause of the 15 November Vulcan failure to the plumbing, not the powerplants. In an update on its blog dated 18 November, the company said that "while work remains to determine cause, preliminary indications are that the low booster thrust was the result of inadequate fuel supply from the vehicle to the BE-4 engines," according to Space.com.

The distinction matters. The loss of 40 Amazon Leo satellites put the spotlight on the two BE-4 engines that power Vulcan's first stage, which are built by Blue Origin. ULA's wording points away from the engines themselves: the propellant was not reaching them in adequate quantity, which is a problem on the vehicle side of the interface. The root cause is still under investigation, and ULA has not said which component or system on the rocket was responsible.

## What this adds

Our [earlier report](/2026/11/18/ula-vulcan-booster-shortfall/) covered ULA CEO Mark Peller's description of a "significant shortfall in booster first stage performance" without naming a cause. This is the first public step beyond that. ULA has also said Vulcan's six solid rocket boosters and the Centaur V upper stage appeared to perform well, and Space.com reports further analysis backs that up.

That matters for the solids in particular. Vulcan's nozzle problems on its October 2024 and February 2026 flights led to a redesign by ULA and Northrop Grumman, and nominal solid performance on this flight suggests the fix held.

## Why it matters for Blue Origin

For Blue Origin the preliminary finding is welcome news. The BE-4 is also the engine for New Glenn, which uses seven or nine of them in its first stage, and New Glenn is itself working toward a return to flight after an explosion during a test on 28 May. A BE-4 fault on Vulcan would have raised questions across both rockets. Preliminary is the operative word, though; the investigation is being overseen by the FAA and no final cause has been published.

## What happens next

ULA has not given a return-to-flight date. After the February solid booster problem, Vulcan was out of action for about nine months, a precedent that will worry customers. As we covered in [our look at the Pentagon fallout](/2026/11/17/vulcan-failure-pentagon-launch-capacity/), the Space Force has already shifted national-security launches to SpaceX, and Amazon has a long Vulcan manifest still ahead of it. How long a fuel-delivery fix takes will decide how much of that stays with ULA.
