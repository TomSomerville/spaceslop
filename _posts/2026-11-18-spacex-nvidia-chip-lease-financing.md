SpaceX is financing a very large pile of Nvidia chips through an off-balance-sheet lease, and the debt behind it may reach investors this week. Yahoo Finance, drawing on Bloomberg reporting, says Goldman Sachs and Apollo Global Management could begin selling pieces of the financing as soon as the week of 17 November.

The structure, as reported: an Apollo-backed special purpose vehicle buys Nvidia's Vera Rubin chips and leases them to SpaceX, so the debt sits outside SpaceX's own books. Headline figures differ by outlet. The chips are worth about $47 billion, while the financing is usually described as roughly $40 billion. Goldman leads an $8.3 billion senior secured loan backed by the hardware, and SpaceX has made a $7 billion prepayment. Separately, Bloomberg reported that SpaceX could start selling $4 billion of bonds as part of a $40 billion financing plan. That detail is unconfirmed.

## Terms that favor the lenders' paperwork, not the lenders

Each batch of chips is leased for about five years, running into 2032, with delivery expected from the first half of 2027. Rent is owed even if the chips are damaged, and there are no early returns. SpaceX is not giving a residual value guarantee, so the lenders carry the risk of what the chips are worth when the lease ends. That is unusual for tech-company chip financing, where such guarantees have become common.

## What the market is saying

SpaceX's debt reportedly rose to nearly $39 billion by 30 September, up from about $23 billion at the end of 2025. Yahoo Finance says SpaceX's five-year credit default swaps widened from about 110 to about 265 basis points between its June IPO and 16 November, a move it translates into roughly 20 percent odds of default over five years. That figure is Yahoo's own calculation. Yahoo also notes it is a portfolio company of funds managed by Apollo affiliates.

One customer, identified by Bloomberg as Anthropic, reportedly supplied about a third of SpaceX's third-quarter revenue under a contract that can be ended with 90 days' notice. That concentration is part of the credit story: the chips are meant to serve an AI compute business, and the lease payments are fixed regardless.

For a launch company, the shift is notable. SpaceX's capital needs are now being set by AI infrastructure as much as by rockets, and its borrowing costs will be watched by the rest of the space sector's investors. Some outlets describe the deal as signed, while the Bloomberg-based accounts describe lenders as still working to sell down portions, so treat terms as reported rather than confirmed by SpaceX.
