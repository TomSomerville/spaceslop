ICEYE, the Finnish company best known for its fleet of small radar-imaging satellites, has made an all-cash offer to buy Avio, the Italian firm behind the Vega rocket family. The bid, announced on 17 November, is €41 per share, a 21.3% premium over Avio's closing price the day before, and values the company at about €2.05 billion.

The offer comes through an Italian subsidiary called Iris 2. Despite the name, it has nothing to do with the EU's IRIS² connectivity constellation. If the deal goes through, ICEYE plans to take Avio off the Milan stock exchange.

## Why a satellite company wants a rocket company

ICEYE's pitch is vertical integration with a sovereignty angle. The combined company would build satellites, launch them on its own rockets, and sell the resulting intelligence to governments. CEO and co-founder Rafal Modrzewski framed it bluntly: "Governments need control of their critical space infrastructure, and Europe needs it most."

The logic runs both ways. ICEYE needs a reliable path to orbit to keep growing its constellation, and Avio gets a guaranteed long-term customer. In its statement, ICEYE said that securing launch capacity is essential for its growth, while long-term visibility on demand is essential for Avio.

That's the SpaceX playbook in miniature: own the launcher, fill it with your own payloads, and stop depending on someone else's manifest.

## Sweeteners and hurdles

ICEYE says it will invest an additional €1 billion in Italy over five years if the acquisition completes. The money would go into manufacturing capacity, R&D, test infrastructure, intellectual property and domestic suppliers, and could create up to 400 jobs. The purchase itself would be paid for with a mix of existing cash and debt. ICEYE closed a €450 million funding round in June.

The bigger obstacle is approval. According to European Spaceflight, the deal needs regulatory clearance involving Italy, France, the UK and the United States, plus sign-off under the EU's Foreign Subsidies Regulation. Avio is a strategic defense and launch asset, so a foreign takeover is unlikely to be waved through quickly.

Avio itself has only said that it received the offer and that its board will begin assessing it. There is no word yet on whether the board will back the bid, and no offer timetable has been published. This story is based on a single report so far. Expect the official filings, and Rome's reaction, to shape what comes next.
