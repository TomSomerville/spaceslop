Verde Technologies, a solar cell maker that started out serving terrestrial energy, has a NASA contract to put its ultra-thin perovskite cells on the outside of the International Space Station, Payload reported on November 23. The flight is part of NASA's Materials International Space Station Experiment (MISSE), planned for no earlier than June 2027. The cells will sit on the exterior for six months and then return to Earth for analysis.

It would be the first space validation of Verde's technology. CEO Jean-Noel Poirier told Payload the company pivoted toward space and high-altitude uses about a year before this mission, driven by demand from satellite builders.

## The pitch

Poirier's argument is about supply. Gallium arsenide cells perform well but have little production capacity. Silicon is plentiful but heavier, less efficient and degrades in space, and most of it comes from China. Verde makes a perovskite-only cell printed on a flexible, lightweight substrate rather than layering perovskite on silicon or glass, as competitors typically do. The cells can be folded or rolled for launch and then deployed into large structures, with partners supplying rigidity. Skipping silicon, the company says, also sidesteps supply-chain issues and lets it serve the U.S. defense market directly.

The market needs "American-made or European-made" solutions that work in space at low cost and at scale, Poirier said.

## What's next

Verde plans to validate the technology fully, then enter the market in stages: satellites and high-altitude drones first, then smallsats, large satellites, constellations and eventually orbital data centers. "The plan for us is to validate the technology completely, then start entering the market in steps," Poirier said.

Six months of exposure on the ISS will test the failure mode that matters most for perovskites in orbit, which is how they hold up over time. A pass would give the company flight heritage, the thing satellite buyers ask for first.
