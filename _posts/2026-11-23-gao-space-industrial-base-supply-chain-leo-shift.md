The Government Accountability Office says the Pentagon's move from a handful of large satellites to big constellations in low Earth orbit is leaning hard on a specialized industrial base, and that the department has not organized itself to manage the resulting supply chain risk. The report, published last week, was required by the fiscal 2025 defense authorization act.

According to DefenseScoop's reading of the report, the Pentagon expects to spend about $33 billion over five years on the shift. That spending competes with other growing sectors for the same semiconductors, electronics, raw materials and manufacturing capacity, which GAO described as "intense competition for critical assets." Defense Daily's headline singles out radiation-hardened processors and optical communications as specific risk areas.

## Three programs, real delays

GAO examined three programs: the Space Development Agency's Proliferated Warfighter Space Architecture (about $15.6 billion over five years), Lockheed Martin's GPS IIIF, and the Resilient Missile Warning and Tracking program in medium Earth orbit (about $8.3 billion over five years).

The Proliferated architecture has missed demonstration deadlines and run into scaling problems, and GAO tied its delivery delays to supply chain disruptions. As of April 2026, officials said bottlenecks and parts availability were still holding up launches. The medium-orbit missile warning effort has seen launch delays caused by printed circuit board shortages.

GAO said it had planned to look at five programs but dropped two, the Space Data Network Backbone and Protected Tactical Satellite Communications-Global, because their program managers had not closely examined their own supply chains.

## No one clearly in charge

The organizational findings are blunter. The Air Force set up an enterprise risk management function through an April memo, but it has no designated leader and its roles are not clearly defined, GAO found. A supply chain risk identification guide issued by Space Systems Command the same month carries no authority outside the Space Force, which leaves the National Reconnaissance Office and the rest of the Air Force outside it.

GAO made three recommendations: that the defense secretary centralize oversight of supply chain gaps across the Department of the Air Force and the NRO, and that the Air Force secretary establish leadership roles within the space acquisition organization and develop protocols for enterprise-level risks. The Defense Department and the Air Force agreed with all three.

The report also repeats a figure from a September study that 94 percent of U.S. satellites on orbit are privately owned, a reminder that the commercial suppliers the Pentagon is counting on are not working only for it.
