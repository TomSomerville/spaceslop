Lockheed Martin says it is reshaping how it builds NASA's Orion crew capsule, moving the program from a development footing to something closer to a production line as it works on the vehicles for Artemis III, IV and V.

According to Interesting Engineering's report on Lockheed's account, the company has added more than 100 technicians and support staff and now runs Orion operations around the clock, on three shifts, seven days a week. Senior engineers are organized into "Rapid Response Teams" that settle problems on the assembly floor immediately instead of pulling main integration crews off their work.

## Testing and reuse

The larger change is in how hardware is tested. Once a design has been qualified, Lockheed says, acceptance testing only needs to confirm that each unit was built correctly, rather than repeating qualification. Orion hardware director Norick put it this way: "Once a design is qualified, acceptance testing can focus on verifying that each unit was built correctly." The company is also reusing flight-qualified hardware from earlier Artemis missions to hold down cost and protect schedule.

## Heat shield supply

The supply chain was a bottleneck too. Upgrades at NASA's Michoud Assembly Facility in Louisiana expanded production of Avcoat, the ablative material for Orion's 16.5-foot heat shield, from roughly four billets a week to more than 20. The shield is designed to withstand about 5,000 degrees Fahrenheit at speeds near 25,000 mph on return from the Moon.

## Where it fits

Artemis I flew uncrewed around the Moon and Artemis II carried four astronauts on a lunar flyby. Per the report, Artemis III is now planned as a crewed orbital rendezvous test in Earth orbit, while Artemis IV is the mission planned to land astronauts on the surface for the first time since Apollo 17. That makes the capsules for III through V the schedule-critical line items on the crew side, even though the lander and suit programs are widely seen as the harder problems.

The figures come from Lockheed's own telling, relayed by one outlet, and the company has not published a total saving or a count of production days removed. Faster material output also does not by itself show that finished capsules will clear integration and certification at the same pace. The report dates to November 6; it surfaced again in Korean-language coverage this week as Lockheed's Orion push drew attention in Seoul.
