The Canadian Space Agency has awarded the University of Saskatchewan a $2.6 million contract to develop Osiris-3, an atmospheric instrument for a small satellite due to launch in 2029. The agency announced the award on X on Tuesday, according to SpaceQ.

Osiris-3 succeeds the OSIRIS instrument that measured ozone from Sweden's Odin satellite from 2001 until earlier this year. It looks at sunlight scattered by the atmosphere along the horizon to track ozone, nitrogen dioxide and aerosols by altitude. The team estimates the optics weigh about 2 kg and draw 15 to 20 watts, small enough for a CubeSat.

The contract covers design, construction, testing and support through mission operations. It was a sole-source award: an advance notice, which closed on August 30, said the university was "the only supplier that can do the work", and estimated a value of $2.5 million to $3 million including options over roughly 36 months.

## The mission

Osiris-3 will fly on CubeSat Mission 1 (CM-1), a 16U satellite planned for a 12-month mission. The university builds the instrument, with Adam Bourassa as principal investigator and Doug Degenstein as co-investigator. The CSA will integrate the payload, handle system-level testing and operations, and buy the launch. A separate contractor will supply the satellite platform. The launch provider has not been named.

The university has also issued a request for quotes for a CM-1 camera system, with bids due December 9, so more subcontracting work is coming. A 2022 CSA grant of $100,000 funded a 6U-sized prototype that flew on a CNES balloon in June 2024.

For Canada's small but active space industry, the award is another step in using CubeSats for science that once required larger satellites, while the SCISAT mission, whose extended operations run to March 31, 2029, continues in parallel.
