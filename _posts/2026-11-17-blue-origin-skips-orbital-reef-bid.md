Blue Origin will not submit its Orbital Reef station in NASA's next round of commercial low Earth orbit awards, according to reporting based on a message CEO Dave Limp sent employees on Thursday, November 12. Ars Technica's Eric Berger was first to report it, per Tech Times' account, and Aviation Week and SpaceNews followed. Blue Origin's public line, as relayed by Aviation Week, is that it is "focusing resources on capabilities that directly advance our lunar projects."

## What it means for the competition

NASA's Commercial LEO Destinations Phase 2 final request for proposals came out October 9, with bids due Tuesday, December 8 and awards expected in spring 2027, around April. NASA plans firm-fixed-price, multiple-award contracts: two or more companies get early development work, then compete again on a task order covering final design, testing, certification and services.

With Blue Origin out, the expected field is Axiom Space, Starlab Space (led by Voyager Technologies, now a joint venture with Airbus) and Vast. Vast has said it plans to launch its Haven-1 station in the first quarter of 2027. GAO lists unfunded agreements with SpaceX and Sierra Space as well, and coverage so far doesn't say whether either will bid. Sierra Space was Blue Origin's main Orbital Reef partner, alongside Thales Alenia Space, Redwire and others. It has not publicly laid out a standalone plan for the station.

Blue Origin received $130 million in the first phase of the program, according to Messier's account. It still holds an unfunded Space Act Agreement with NASA to mature technology and build hardware for a crewed vehicle, and nothing in the reporting calls the exit permanent. Skipping one round is not the same as cancelling the station.

## The money problem

The timing sits against a tight budget picture. GAO said in June that NASA expected $1 billion to $1.5 billion for fiscal 2026 through 2031 to fund two or more agreements, and NASA officials said in March that this "may only be sufficient to support one commercial space station." A House appropriations bill proposes $400 million for the program, up from $300 million in fiscal 2026, which Vast CEO Max Haot and Starlab CEO Marshall Smith argued could pay for two stations. The Senate's S. 933 would require NASA to solicit two. NASA Watch editor Keith Cowing wrote that projected resources support one station at most.

One fewer bidder doesn't solve the funding gap, but it does make a two-station award easier to staff.

## Why the Moon

Blue Origin's lunar work is already large: its Blue Moon landers are central to NASA's Artemis and Moon Base plans, and the company is still trying to get New Glenn flying again after a May hot-fire explosion damaged its Florida pad. CNBC reported on November 5 that the return to flight had slipped to January 2027. A company stretched across a rebuilt pad, lander development and a station would have to pick, and Limp appears to have picked.
