Germany's planned military satellite network in low Earth orbit would cost about 15 billion euros, according to a report in the business daily Handelsblatt that cites three people familiar with the plan. The German outlet Telepolis summarized the report on Wednesday. Neither the figure nor the design has been officially confirmed.

The number is well above earlier estimates. A January report had put the program at 8 to 10 billion euros, but that range excluded value-added tax and operating costs. The new figure reportedly includes both VAT and ten years of operations, and Telepolis notes the breakdown is not final.

## Size and builders

Handelsblatt describes a constellation of about 600 satellites. An earlier report from netzpolitik.org had cited 100 to 200, so the scale is still unsettled. The full network is not expected before 2030, and Berliner Zeitung reported in March that a first phase of up to 40 satellites could be up by 2029.

The builder is expected to be a consortium of Rheinmetall, OHB and Airbus. The three companies have already set up a joint venture, OHB Rheinmetall Space Networks GmbH, in Bremen, and a contract could be signed within weeks, Telepolis reports.

## A bridge from Telesat

Until the German constellation exists, the Bundeswehr is reported to plan on satellites from Canada's Telesat as a stopgap, a detail Telepolis attributes to Golem. That arrangement has its own schedule risk: Telesat has pushed the start of its Lightspeed global service to the end of the second quarter of 2028, its second delay this year, with two test satellites due to launch in June 2027.

## Political hurdles

The parliamentary budget committee is expected to take up the matter in the first quarter of 2027, and the submission has to show what the 15 billion euros covers. Some budget lawmakers, including members of the governing coalition, have warned that a lack of competition inflates prices. A single joint bid removes the leverage of threatening to award the work to a rival.

The program is a sign of how European governments are trying to build sovereign military communications in orbit rather than rely on U.S. providers, though the sources here frame it mainly as a cost and procurement story. The numbers will likely move again before parliament votes.
