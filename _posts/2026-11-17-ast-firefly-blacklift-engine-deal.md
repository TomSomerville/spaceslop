AST SpaceMobile filed a prospectus supplement dated 17 November that spells out how it will pay Firefly Aerospace for rocket engines and the right to build more of them. The satellite operator plans to hand Firefly 10 million Class A shares directly, with no underwriter, and says it receives no cash from the placement. The shares are consideration under the license agreement, not a fundraise.

## What the deal covers

The agreements were signed on 6 November. AST gets a perpetual license to manufacture Firefly's Miranda engine and the vacuum-optimized Vira variant. Firefly will supply engines for the first three rockets and provide engineering support over a 36-month term. According to the filing, AST pays $250 million for services over three years and $250 million for the hardware. Manufacturing Dive reports Firefly will deliver 36 engines in total, 27 Miranda and nine Vira.

The license itself is about $800 million, made up of $400 million in cash plus an estimated 10 million to 14 million shares. The final count depends on a forward volume-weighted average price calculation, and any shares beyond the first 10 million will be offered later in a separate registered direct offering. The filing cites a last sale price of $28.58 on 16 November. Of the 10 million shares, only 2.4 million can be sold by Firefly immediately; the rest are locked up, with sales opening at six months, one year and three years.

## The rocket

The engines are meant for Blacklift, a 20-ton reusable heavy-lift rocket AST plans to build to put its BlueBird satellites into low Earth orbit. Manufacturing Dive says it could carry up to five BlueBirds per launch from late 2028. The filing is blunt about the risk: development, testing, regulatory approvals and launches all face uncertainty and possible delay or failure.

Firefly CEO Jason Kim called it a "win-win", saying AST gains control of its own launch schedule while Firefly gets funds to expand rocket production. Firefly says the money will go to hiring, long-lead materials, equipment and new test bays at its Rocket Ranch site in Briggs, Texas.

## Why it matters

A satellite operator buying engine rights to build its own launcher is a notable move at a moment when launch supply is tight. Vulcan is grounded after its 15 November failure, and Blue Origin's New Glenn has been out of service since a May pad explosion. Owning the means of launch is one answer to that. It is also a very large bet for a company whose core business is selling direct-to-phone service, on a vehicle that is still a plan.
