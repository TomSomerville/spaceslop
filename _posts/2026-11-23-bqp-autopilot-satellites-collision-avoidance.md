BQP, a small simulation company based in Syracuse, New York, is pitching software it calls an "autopilot for satellites": code that lets a spacecraft decide for itself when a debris-avoidance maneuver is worth making. Fortune reported on the company on Monday, noting it has raised $8 million in total and is heading to the International Space Station for a real-environment test.

## The cost problem

Fortune cites NASA figures that put the yearly cost of space junk to U.S. satellites at about $58 million, borne mostly by military and civil spacecraft. A single evasive maneuver costs roughly $1 million, largely in fuel. Founder and CEO Abhishek Chopra, who holds an aerospace engineering PhD from Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute, argues that operators often swerve out of caution because they lack fast, precise ways to judge a risk. He told Fortune that current monitoring is still, in his words, "We're still writing down on paper."

The Space Force tracks about 60,000 objects and sends collision warnings to satellite operators worldwide free of charge. What BQP says it adds is speed. The company claims it can simulate every object in orbit in "under 30 minutes", a job it says would take about 40 days with current tools. BQP describes its method as quantum-inspired: it borrows techniques from quantum computing but runs on conventional chips such as Nvidia GPUs. Chopra says the speedup over Orekit, an open-source orbit tool, has grown from the 250x the company announced in September to as much as 2,000x. These are the company's own claims; Fortune's piece does not describe independent verification.

## Funding and testing

BQP started in India in 2020 and now works with the Space Force. In July it won its first federal contract, a small-business research award from SpaceWERX, the Space Force's innovation arm. That first phase is complete, and the company is seeking a second phase with Space Force units. IBM Ventures invested in September, which Chopra called "almost like a stamp of approval", and at least one more corporate investor has joined the round. Contracted and committed revenue is up eightfold since BQP's 2025 seed round.

On the technical side, the software has finished ground testing on a partner company's hardware and is set to fly to the ISS to be tried in the actual space environment.

## Policy backdrop

The pitch lands amid a fight over who provides civil space traffic services. The Commerce Department's TraCSS pilot, meant to offer a civilian alternative to Space Force data, has cost almost $200 million. The White House proposed shelving it again this year, while Senate appropriators proposed $50 million to keep it going. Whether operators or governments end up paying for better conjunction analysis is still unsettled, and startups like BQP are betting that the answer includes onboard autonomy.
