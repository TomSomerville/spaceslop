Space Systems Command is lining up the follow-on to the contract that keeps the GPS ground system running, and it wants the new deal to do more than keep the lights on. A sources-sought notice dated Nov. 20 describes the Sustaining Position, Navigation and Timing (PNT) and Integrating with Development to Enhance Readiness contract, or SPIDER, with an award targeted for mid-2028, DefenseScoop reported.

## What the Space Force wants

SPIDER would succeed GPS Control Segment Sustainment II (GCS II), which Lockheed Martin won in 2019. The Space Force later added three option years that carry the incumbent's work through 2028. The notice frames SPIDER as a shift away from pure hardware and software sustainment toward a combined sustainment and continuous-development model.

The structure is an indefinite-delivery, indefinite-quantity (IDIQ) contract with a five-year base and an optional second five-year ordering period, executed through multiple task orders. The command cites the complexity and mission-critical nature of the work as the reason for that approach.

The notice lists six task areas: systems engineering support for worldwide GPS sustainment; integrating ground-segment hardware and software upgrades; maintenance at control stations; continuous cyber threat monitoring; integrated logistics support for ground systems; and management of government-furnished property networks across sites. The contract must also support the GPS IIIF follow-on satellites, oversee civil navigation signals and help keep the system resilient against electronic warfare and cyberattack through regular software updates. Most of the work would happen at Schriever Space Force Base in Colorado and Vandenberg Space Force Base in California.

## What happens next

A sources-sought notice is a market survey, not a solicitation, and no award has been made. Industry responses are due Jan. 4, 2027. A Lockheed Martin spokesperson told DefenseScoop the company plans to respond. Lockheed also recently closed out its GPS III satellite line: the tenth and final GPS III satellite launched in April, with GPS IIIF next.

The significance is the ground segment. Satellites get the headlines, but the control segment is what lets new signals and anti-jamming features actually reach users, and the Space Force is signaling it wants a vendor on a continuous-upgrade footing rather than a maintenance cadence. Whether that opens the door to competitors against the incumbent will be clearer once the January responses are in.
