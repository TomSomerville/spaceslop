Atomic-6, a startup that makes impact-resistant tiles for satellites, has published test results suggesting the same material could one day protect astronauts on spacewalks.

At Texas A&M University's Hypervelocity Impact Laboratory, the company fired a 3 mm aluminum sphere at roughly 7.2 km/s (about 16,100 mph) at a ballistic torso, twice. Behind a Space Armor Lite tile, the torso showed no visible damage or penetration, and the back of the tile did not bulge, crack or shed material. Behind a 4 mm solid aluminum plate, which Atomic-6 uses as a stand-in for current spacesuit protection, the projectile went straight through and left fragments in the torso.

## What the test does and doesn't show

Atomic-6 itself is upfront about the limits. A stationary torso is not a spacesuit, and the tests say nothing about injury outcomes or whether a suit would hold pressure after a hit. Next up are tests with curved tiles and different impact angles, and the company says it is in early talks with spacesuit makers about where armor could fit without restricting movement.

The pitch is mostly about thickness. Traditional metal Whipple shields, which break up an incoming particle across a gap, need four to six inches of standoff, far too bulky for a suit. CEO Trevor Smith says Space Armor is under an inch thick. The tiles come in a version that passes radio signals, so they can cover antennas, and one that blocks them, at the same cost and weight.

The company sells two grades. Lite, the version tested here, stops objects up to 3 mm. Max targets objects up to 12.5 mm and is aimed at human-rated spacecraft and habitats. Debris of this size is a worry because it is too small to track from the ground yet fast enough to do real damage.

Last month a Lite tile flew on Portal Space Systems' Starburst-1 spacecraft, mainly to confirm it survived launch, and it did. No orbital impact test is planned. Smith said arranging one is not practical. He also noted that a 9 mm handgun round went through a tile at a local range, but the armor is built for speeds around Mach 10, and its performance improves as the projectile gets faster.

Spacesuit makers and the commercial station developers will be the ones to watch. A tile that works on a bench and survives launch is a long way from one certified for a pressurized suit, but this is the first hint of Atomic-6 moving from satellites toward crew protection.
