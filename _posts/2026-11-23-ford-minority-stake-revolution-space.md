Ford Motor Company is going back into space hardware. On November 23 the automaker said its subsidiary Industrial Platform LLC has signed an agreement to take a minority stake in Revolution Space, a Boston-headquartered prime contractor for space systems built around in-space propulsion.

Financial terms were not disclosed, and the size of the stake was not given. Closing is subject to customary conditions, including regulatory approvals.

## What Ford is bringing

The pitch is manufacturing. Ford's release argues that the satellite market is shifting from hand-built, one-off spacecraft toward repeatable, automated production, and that Ford's experience building complex products in volume fits that shift. Chief Strategy Officer Marin Gjaja said in the statement: "Ford's strength is building complex products in large volumes, something today's growing satellite market requires."

The companies say the goal is to industrialize Revolution Space's next-generation spacecraft so they can serve "dynamic" missions and proliferated constellations, meaning large fleets of satellites. The release gives no spacecraft model names, production volumes or timeline. Revolution Space says its systems can operate from very low Earth orbit up to geosynchronous altitude, across cislunar space and beyond.

Ford framed the deal as a limited-capital bet in a less cyclical business than cars, part of a broader push to diversify into growth markets where it has an advantage.

## Who Revolution Space is

The company describes itself as 100 percent U.S. operated, with offices in Boston, California, Washington D.C., Albuquerque and Denver. Its chairman is Daniel S. Goldin, the longest-serving NASA Administrator. The release quotes him saying Revolution Space "has demonstrated the ability to meet the performance requirements for the coming generations," without elaborating.

## Context

Ford is not a newcomer to the field. Its release recalls that from the 1960s into the 1970s the company built communications and weather satellites for NASA, the military and NATO, that its Philco-Ford unit was prime contractor for NASA's Mission Control Center in Houston from 1963, and that its aerospace division won a $235 million contract in 1976 to build seven INTELSAT satellites.

The deal lands as satellite makers race to scale up for large constellations, and as automotive-style production lines become a recurring theme in spacecraft manufacturing. The release cites World Economic Forum projections of the space economy growing from about $600 billion today to $1.8 trillion by 2035. Those are the companies' chosen market figures, not an independent assessment. What the partnership will actually produce, and when, remains to be seen.
