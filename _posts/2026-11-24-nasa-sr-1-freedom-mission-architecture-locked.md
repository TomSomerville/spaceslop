NASA said on Tuesday that SR-1 Freedom, its planned nuclear-electric spacecraft bound for Mars, has completed a major mission design review and locked in key elements of the vehicle. The review took place in October at NASA's Glenn Research Center in Cleveland, with experts from the agency, the Department of Energy and industry. NASA describes the result as a move from early concept toward a defined design, with launch targeted for late 2028.

## What the spacecraft will do

SR-1 will use both fission and solar power. Solar arrays carry the spacecraft through cruise to Mars, while the reactor is used to demonstrate nuclear electric propulsion in deep space. NASA says it will be the first fission-powered spacecraft to travel beyond Earth orbit. After leaving Earth orbit the reactor ramps up, runs for an initial period and then shuts down while the spacecraft collects and transmits performance data. Once the vehicle delivers its payload to Mars orbit, the reactor restarts for more data.

That payload is SkyFall, a capsule carrying three next-generation helicopters. NASA says they will study Martian geology and climate history, explore new terrain and search for subsurface ice.

## Design changes

The spacecraft's main body is the Power and Propulsion Element, with a boom connecting the reactor to it. The boom is now shorter than in early illustrations, which NASA says saves weight and simplifies assembly. Improved shielding and design changes let the reactor sit closer to the rest of the spacecraft. The vehicle will also carry larger X-band antenna dishes for deep-space communication.

Several parameters remain open. NASA says the number of radiator panels, the output of the nuclear power module and how long the reactor will operate are still under evaluation, and it published no numerical specifications.

## Contract

Separately, NASA awarded a $39.3 million contract to Johns Hopkins University's Applied Physics Laboratory for the nuclear power module's heat rejection system. APL is working with commercial partner Advanced Cooling Technologies on heat pipe and composite radiator technologies. The award was made under the Aerospace Research, Development, and Engineering Support II contract.

## Why it matters

Heat rejection is one of the hard problems for any space reactor, since radiators drive mass and layout, so the open radiator-panel count is worth watching. NASA also ties the work to its Moon base plans, noting that future missions and lunar surface operations will need more power than current systems provide, especially where sunlight is limited.

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman first introduced the concept in March at the agency's Ignition event, with a goal of launching in time for the next Mars launch window. SR-1 program director Steve Sinacore said: "This mission will be the first freight run on the railroad of the solar system."
