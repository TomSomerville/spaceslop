The Pentagon has raised the combined maximum value of its three National Security Space Launch (NSSL) Phase 3 Lane 2 contracts by $985 million, modifying the deals held by SpaceX, United Launch Alliance and Blue Origin. The change was announced in the Department of War's contract listings this week.

The figure is a ceiling, not a payment. Reporting on the announcement says no funds were obligated when the modifications were signed, and the notice does not say how the $985 million is divided among the three companies. It also does not identify any new missions or payloads. The modifications also extend the period of performance through April 2033, with work at Cape Canaveral, Kennedy Space Center and Vandenberg.

## What was modified

Each company's contract got its own modification: SpaceX's FA8811-25-D-B002 (P00006), ULA's FA8811-25-D-B003 (P00004) and Blue Origin's FA8811-25-D-B005 (P00006). Space Systems Command's Space Access office at Los Angeles Air Force Base is the contracting activity.

Lane 2 covers the bulk of national security launches, and the contracts are firm-fixed-price, indefinite-delivery arrangements. In practice that means the ceiling sets how much the government could order, while the real money arrives later as individual task orders for specific missions.

## Context

When the Lane 2 contracts were awarded in April 2025, their anticipated values were about $5.92 billion for SpaceX across 28 missions, $5.37 billion for ULA across 19 missions, and $2.39 billion for Blue Origin across seven, roughly $13.7 billion for 54 missions in total. The original ordering period covers fiscal years 2025 to 2029. One report cites a June 2025 Government Accountability Office estimate of about 85 national security launches in fiscal 2025 to 2029, roughly double the prior five-year stretch.

The timing is hard to ignore. The modifications surfaced shortly after ULA's Vulcan suffered its second failure in a year, which we covered in [our earlier article on Vulcan and Pentagon launch capacity](/2026/11/17/vulcan-failure-pentagon-launch-capacity/). Nothing in the announcement links the two, and a ceiling change on contracts of this size is a contracting action that could have been in the works for some time, so the coincidence should not be read as cause and effect.

What the move does give the Space Force is headroom. If Vulcan stays grounded and missions need to be reshuffled between providers, the contracts can absorb more work without a new competition. Whether that work flows to SpaceX, to ULA once it returns to flight, or eventually to Blue Origin depends on task orders that have not been announced. One report notes that Blue Origin has received none so far because New Glenn is not yet certified for these missions.
