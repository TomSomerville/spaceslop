Japan will recruit a new class of astronaut candidates. The Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology (MEXT) announced on November 27 that JAXA plans to select "a few" people, the agency's first astronaut call in roughly six years by one outlet's count (other outlets say five, measured from the 2021 call).

## The schedule

According to Japanese coverage of the announcement, JAXA is expected to publish the formal requirements and hold an information session around April 2027. Applications would open around June 2027 and run for about three months. Results are expected in autumn 2028.

Detailed eligibility has not been released. Both outlets report the policy is to repeat the 2021 approach, which dropped the requirement for a natural-science degree or career. The 2008 call, by contrast, required a four-year university degree and a science background.

## What happened last time

The 2021 call drew a record 4,127 applicants, against 963 in 2008. Joho Todai reports that 2,266 passed document screening, and the field was then narrowed through several rounds to two candidates, announced in February 2023: Suwa and Yoneda. Both were certified as astronauts in October 2024 after basic training. Suwa is reportedly assigned to SpaceX's Crew-14 mission to the International Space Station, expected to launch after March 2027.

JAXA has said since 2021 that it intends to recruit roughly every five years, and this call fits that rhythm.

## Why now

MEXT's framing points to the 2030s. Human activity is expanding from low Earth orbit toward the Moon, and the ministry cites Artemis and Japan's contribution of a pressurized lunar rover as part of that plan. Candidates picked in 2028 would finish about a year and a half of basic training in time to be available for those missions.

Astronaut Akihiko Hoshide said in a JAXA statement that the agency is looking for people with curiosity and a spirit of inquiry who can work as part of an international team.

For the wider industry, the call is a small but concrete signal that Tokyo expects to keep its seats in the Artemis programme and on commercial stations, and wants a deeper pool of crew to fill them.
