Our earlier report covers the Flight 15 ship catch itself. This piece looks at what is still unproven. Space.com reports SpaceX spokesperson Dan Huot called the catch "the holy grail of spaceflight" and said "now everything changes." Details of why the booster missed its own catch vary: Space.com says it diverted to the Gulf after a low-fuel alarm, while Spaceflight Now reported a guidance-system call and a later correction, and SpaceX is still investigating.

## Reuse is the open question

SpaceX says catching both stages will become standard on operational flights, enabling quick inspection and reflight. That has not been shown. Super Heavy boosters have flown twice, with months between flights, and no Ship has been reflown. Elon Musk has said he wants to reuse both stages, possibly including the Flight 15 ship. He has also targeted 10,000 Starship launches a year by 2030, more than one an hour on average, which Space.com ties to plans for a 100,000-satellite Starlink V3 network and a proposed AI data-center constellation ten times larger. Today's Starlink has about 11,000 satellites.

## The Moon and refueling

NASA chose Starship as the first crewed lunar lander, with a landing near the south pole planned for Artemis IV, targeted for 2028. SpaceX still has to install a crew cabin and life support. Per a NASA presentation last month, it is on track to finish "Cabin 1", a full-scale ground prototype, by year end.

The biggest technical gap is refueling. Ship cannot leave Earth orbit fully fueled, so tanker Ships must fill a depot with cryogenic liquid oxygen and methane. Outside experts have suggested as many as 22 tanker flights per mission; Musk has estimated six to eight. A first transfer test is expected relatively soon, according to Space.com. If SpaceX is not ready, NASA could turn to Blue Origin's Blue Moon lander, which it has also contracted.
