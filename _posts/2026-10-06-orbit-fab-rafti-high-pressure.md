Orbit Fab has finished developing a high-pressure version of its RAFTI refuelling port, widening the range of satellites that could one day be topped up in orbit. The company announced RAFTI High Pressure (RAFTI HP) on October 6, 2026, saying orders are open and it is ready to start delivering units.

## What it is

RAFTI is Orbit Fab's interface that replaces the single-use fill-and-drain valve on a satellite's propulsion system. It supports fuelling on the ground and, later, refuelling in orbit. The original design targets lower-pressure propellants such as hydrazine and is already baselined on a growing number of satellites, according to the company; the release names none of them.

RAFTI HP was designed, built and qualified by Orbit Fab's UK team at Harwell, England, and carries a maximum expected operating pressure of 345 bar (5,000 psi). It is qualified for xenon, krypton and argon, the propellants used by electric thrusters, and for nitrous oxide and ethane, which pressurise themselves as chemical propellants.

Qualification covered industry-standard environmental, pressure, leak and functional tests under SPITFIRE, a programme funded by the UK Space Agency's National Space Innovation Programme. Testing at Orbit Fab's Harwell fluids laboratory involved the University of Southampton, MDA UK, the Satellite Applications Catapult and the University of Surrey.

## Why it matters

Refuelling has so far been easiest to imagine for conventional chemical systems. Many modern satellites, however, use electric propulsion with gases stored at high pressure, and a port that cannot handle that pressure cannot serve them. With RAFTI HP, Orbit Fab can offer a single family of interfaces across both kinds of system. The company argues operators can fit it for ground fuelling now with little added cost, mass, volume or complexity, and keep the option of in-orbit refuelling open.

"Satellites don't retire because their hardware fails – they retire because they run out of fuel," said Jacob Geer, managing director of Orbit Fab in Europe.

## What's next

The first flight unit is set to launch on the Shilling in-orbit demonstration in late 2026. The payload includes xenon and instrumentation to verify performance in orbit. The mission is named for Beatrice "Tilly" Shilling, a British engineer who fixed a fuel-flow problem in the Spitfire's engine during the Second World War.

The release does not name customers for RAFTI HP, and it gives no prices or delivery dates beyond saying orders are open. The in-orbit demonstration will be the real test of whether the interface behaves as qualified on the ground.
