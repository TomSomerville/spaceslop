Sateliot, the Barcelona-based operator of a low Earth orbit network for connecting Internet-of-Things devices, announced on October 1 that it launched five new satellites on schedule. The mission, called Draconis, brings the number of Sateliot satellites launched to date to 11.

According to the company, the extra spacecraft roughly double the average number of daily passes over any given location and cut revisit times in half. That matters for an IoT network, where sensors on pipelines, livestock or shipping containers send small bursts of data and can only talk to the constellation when a satellite is overhead.

## What changed from the 2024 batch

The five satellites follow four launched in 2024, and Sateliot says they carry a series of upgrades: an optimised payload, a more reliable and efficient onboard data storage system, an upgraded onboard computer and attitude control system, and more radiation-resistant memory. Each is a 10-kilogram spacecraft measuring 20 x 10 x 35 centimetres, with a stated five-year operational lifespan, flying at 510 kilometres. They were built by Alén Space, and Exolaunch handled launch integration. The release does not name the rocket.

Sateliot's pitch is a store-and-forward architecture built on the 3GPP Release 17 non-terrestrial network (NTN) standard, so standard commercial NB-IoT devices can connect from space without special hardware. The company describes its network as the first LEO constellation based on that standard and says it is part of a planned constellation of more than 500 satellites that will move toward higher-capacity services and direct-to-device connectivity.

CEO and co-founder Jaume Sanpera said Draconis "is a clear reflection of where the company stands today" and that "we have successfully validated the technology." Sateliot plans to launch its next-generation Tritó satellites in 2027, aimed at 5G NR direct-to-device service.

Eleven satellites is a long way from 500, and the release gives no financing or customer figures for the build-out. But doubling coverage with a second batch on a regular cadence is the kind of step IoT operators need to show before roaming deals with mobile carriers turn into recurring revenue.
