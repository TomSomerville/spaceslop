The Federal Aviation Administration is in the middle of a week of public scoping meetings on SpaceX's request to dramatically raise how often it can fly Starship from Boca Chica, Texas. According to the Tesla North account of the FAA's updated notice, all five sessions have been moved online, and the written comment deadline has been extended from November 30 to December 7.

## What is being proposed

SpaceX is currently authorized for up to 25 launches and 50 landings a year at the site, the latter counting both Starships and Super Heavy boosters. The FAA's programmatic environmental impact statement (PEIS) would look at two steps beyond that, as reported by Tesla North:

- **Phase 1:** up to 100 launches and 200 landings a year, on any day and at any hour.
- **Phase 2:** up to 456 launches and 912 landings a year, with launches limited to weekdays but permitted at any hour. The FAA describes this as a mature program reached over several years.

The landing totals include vehicles that could arrive from elsewhere. The review is also meant to cover expanded landing and ocean disposal areas and the temporary airspace closures that come with them, and to compare the proposals against simply keeping today's limits.

## The meetings

The virtual sessions run on Central Time, require registration, and offer Spanish translation. Each opens with a 30-minute FAA presentation followed by oral comments of up to three minutes. The listed slots were November 17 (1-3 p.m. and 5:30-7:30 p.m.), November 18 (5:30-7:30 p.m.) and November 19 (1-3 p.m. and 5:30-7:30 p.m.). Written comments go to docket FAA-2026-12808 on regulations.gov. The report did not say why the meetings moved online.

## Why it matters

This is the scoping stage, so nothing is approved. A draft PEIS will come later for another round of public review, and a final decision after that. But the numbers show where SpaceX wants Starship to go: 456 launches a year would be more than one a day on average, which is the sort of cadence a fully reusable system needs to make its economics work, and well beyond any other launch site's current rate. Noise, air quality, wildlife and coastal impacts around the Rio Grande Valley are the issues the FAA says it will weigh.

One caveat on sourcing: the FAA's own project page and the Federal Register notice were not accessible when this was written, so the figures above rely on a single secondary report and should be checked against the docket.
