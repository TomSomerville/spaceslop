Sierra Space announced on Tuesday that it has finished a drop-test campaign for Ghost, its family of reentry vehicles meant to carry cargo down from orbit to a chosen spot on Earth. The tests took place at the Dugway Proving Ground in Utah as part of U.S. Space Command's Apollo Nexus exercise program.

According to the company, the first drop was made from more than 38,000 feet and the second from more than 67,000 feet. Sierra Space says both were successful. The campaign covered two configurations of the vehicle.

## Two variants

Ghost XL is the large version, developed in support of the Air Force Research Laboratory's REGAL program. Sierra Space says it is designed to move payloads of 5 to 10 tons anywhere in the world within 90 minutes.

Micro Ghost is a newly named small variant for payloads of 250 pounds or less. The company pitches it for tactical, time-sensitive delivery to places where access, weight limits, GPS availability or visibility are a problem.

Chief executive Dan Jablonsky framed the use cases in military terms: a downed pilot in the Pacific who needs search-and-rescue supplies, or an island that needs a containerized desalination plant after a disaster. Chad Faria, a Space Command exercise planner, said the exercise showed how commercial technology can support operational readiness.

## What the tests do and don't show

A drop from the stratosphere is a long way from a return from orbit. The release does not describe any test at orbital velocity, and it does not say how the vehicles were released or what altitude profile they flew beyond the two drop heights. The 90-minute delivery figure is a design target, not something these drops measured. The hard parts of the concept, deorbit, heating during reentry and precision landing without GPS, remain to be shown in flight.

The company says Ghost is being developed to land cargo accurately on short timelines without ground infrastructure or GPS navigation during landing.

Sierra Space is best known for the Dream Chaser spaceplane, but it now describes itself as a defense-tech space company, and Ghost fits that repositioning. Whether the 90-minute promise survives real reentry is the test that matters, and the announcement gives no date for it.
