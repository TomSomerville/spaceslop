Amazon is sticking to its plan to start initial Leo broadband service this year, even after a ULA Vulcan failure destroyed 40 of its satellites earlier this week. The company told Fierce Network it already has enough satellites in orbit for continuous coverage across certain latitudes and expects to begin offering service "within weeks in select areas."

The loss, which [we covered earlier]({% post_url 2026-11-17-vulcan-failure-pentagon-launch-capacity %}), came when Vulcan suffered low booster performance just after liftoff. ULA's early findings, as quoted by Fierce, say the second stage performed well but could not recover from the booster problem.

## What Amazon says it has

Amazon says it has launched nearly 400 satellites so far and built more than 1,000, with hundreds flight-ready. Fierce notes that Amazon also cites a figure of more than 200 successfully launched satellites, and the article does not explain the gap between the two numbers or say whether the 40 lost satellites are included in the larger count. Treat the exact on-orbit total as unsettled.

Amazon said it is in close contact with ULA, will support the Vulcan investigation, and wants a return to flight as soon as possible. It also said it is exploring all options to keep its deployment schedule. It has not said whether it will sign additional launch contracts with other providers.

## What's next

A batch of 36 satellites is set to launch as soon as next week, and Fierce reports a November 23 launch from French Guiana on Arianespace. Amazon originally targeted a mid-2026 commercial debut and delayed it earlier this year. In March, VP Chris Weber said initial coverage would target latitude bands in both hemispheres where ground infrastructure is ready.

The Vulcan loss hurts more over the long run than for the first service start. Fierce reports the rocket was meant to carry more than 1,500 Leo satellites across 38 missions, so a grounded Vulcan leaves a hole in the deployment plan. The regulatory clock is distant but real: the FCC requires Amazon to deploy its initial 3,232-satellite first-generation constellation by July 30, 2029.

Note that the headline on Fierce's piece says "year-end," while the body says "later this year" and "within weeks." No specific service start date has been given.
