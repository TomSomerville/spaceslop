Inflection Space Systems, a Y Combinator-backed startup, has booked its first orbital flight. Pax, a 16U cubesat of about 36 kilograms, is slated to launch no earlier than 2028 on a rideshare arranged through Exolaunch, according to an exclusive from Payload.

Pax is a stepping stone. Its job is to give flight heritage to the avionics, sensors, power systems and structures that Inflection plans to reuse in Exponent, a standardized satellite bus. Exponent is a much bigger vehicle: up to 300 kg at launch, with room for 100 kg of payload and 1.5 kilowatts of power.

## Selling buses like commodities

The pitch is price and volume. Inflection quotes $1.5 million for a single Exponent, falling to $700,000 apiece for orders of 100 or more. The factory is designed for autonomous assembly and round-the-clock "lights-out" production, and CEO and cofounder Alex Lisso describes an "aggressive approach to vertical integration," with the company building its own hardware and flight software so each part can be mass-produced.

Lisso's thesis is that demand from orbital data centers, Golden Dome missile defense and satellite communications constellations will require far more spacecraft than the industry has ever built, and that satellite manufacturing has so far been "high-mix, low-volume." Whether that demand materializes at the scale he expects is the open question, and the prices are the company's own targets rather than signed contracts.

## What Pax will carry

The cubesat will fly a radiation testbed from The Radiation Team and Indiana University's Center for Reliable & Trusted Electronics, which stays on for a three-year mission after commissioning. A second, unnamed customer will fly a technology demonstration. Inflection says Pax will test its manufacturing readiness level as well as its technology readiness level, which is a useful admission: building one good spacecraft is a different problem from building hundreds.

The schedule is tight for a company at this stage. Inflection aims to finish Exponent's critical design review and build a first unit by the end of 2027, then ramp production in 2028, the same year Pax is due to fly. That means the bus design will be frozen before the flight data that is supposed to justify it comes back.

Inflection joins a crowded field of bus makers chasing the same constellation and defense demand, and the low-cost, high-volume lane is where several larger players are already positioned. A launch slot booked three years out is a modest first proof point, but it is a concrete one.
