Asynchronics, a Warsaw-based space software startup, has raised a seed round of about $3.1 million (€2.82 million) to expand a catalogue of digital twins of spacecraft hardware. The round was led by b2venture, with Movens Capital, HEARTFELT and Hard2beat also taking part. Payload dated the announcement to November 17; The Recursive reported it two days later.

The company was founded in 2023 by Adam Chikha (CEO) and Serge Barral (CTO), both Airbus veterans. It has about 11 employees and plans to grow to 24.

## What it sells

The core product is NeXosim, an open-source simulation engine that lets teams model a spacecraft, or an entire mission, in software before anything flies. The engine itself is free. Revenue comes from a marketplace of digital twins of individual components, which builders can drop into their designs for a small fee.

The pitch is aimed at the part of the market that has grown fastest: startups and small or mid-sized space companies that cannot afford the in-house simulation tooling large primes have built over decades. "Smaller companies or mid-sized companies do not have access to this kind of simulation tooling," Chikha told The Recursive. K2 Space and Infinite Orbits are named as users.

Since 2024 Asynchronics has worked with European component maker NewSpace Systems on twins of its hardware. Chikha said that in a recent test, the real parts behaved "identical" to their digital counterparts. That is a founder's claim, reported by one outlet, and not an independent validation.

## What the money is for

Asynchronics says it will add about 50 components over the next two years, focusing on popular categories such as reaction wheels, solar array drive assemblies and power control and distribution units. It also plans visual design representations in its software and an AI tool for generating models, aimed at cutting the time to build a twin from months to days.

## Why it matters

As satellite production moves toward high volume, test campaigns on hardware become a bottleneck, and failures found late are expensive. Better simulation of off-the-shelf parts is one way to catch integration problems earlier. Whether a marketplace model can sustain a business, with the engine given away, is the open question; the seed round buys roughly two years to find out.
