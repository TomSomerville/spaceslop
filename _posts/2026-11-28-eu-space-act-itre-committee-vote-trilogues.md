The European Parliament's Industry, Research and Energy (ITRE) committee approved its position on the EU Space Act on 19 November, with 73 votes in favour, one against and two abstentions, according to EUperspectives. Other outlets cite a slightly different tally of 72-1. Either way, the margin gives Parliament a negotiating mandate and clears the way for talks with EU governments.

## What changed

The committee's text is lighter than the European Commission's original proposal. Smaller companies, research institutions and universities would face reduced requirements, including exemptions from environmental reporting. The environmental footprint certificate that operators would otherwise need for authorisation is scrapped, the authorisation deadline is shortened, and overlapping procedures between national authorities are trimmed.

Rapporteur Elena Donazzan said lawmakers "simplified things" while keeping the act's three pillars: safety, resilience and environmental sustainability. Her team worked through nearly 4,000 amendments to reach the compromise.

## What's still open

The Council of the EU has not caught up. The Irish presidency circulated a revised compromise text on 29 October, but The European Post reports that every delegation still holds scrutiny reservations in the Council's working party on space. Four disputes remain: alignment with the NIS2 cybersecurity directive, the certification deadline (12 months in the Commission draft, six in the committee draft, and three in a proposal from the Renew group), the treatment of non-EU operators, and sustainability duties such as debris mitigation.

If the Council settles its position, trilogue negotiations among Parliament, Council and Commission could start as early as December. The Lithuanian presidency, which takes over in January 2027, would inherit the hardest compromises. The same outlet judges that the rules are unlikely to apply before 2029 or 2030.

## Why it matters outside Europe

Under the draft, third-country operators would enter through a Union Repository of Space Activities rather than the EU certification route. The European Post notes that those access rules could set market entry costs for US constellation operators for about a decade, and says the US Office of Space Commerce is reviewing the text.

For European industry, the political pitch is competitiveness. EUperspectives quotes Renew's Christophe Grudler calling for EU space funding to rise from €15 billion to between €45 billion and €60 billion in the next long-term budget, and notes that the US attempted 181 orbital launches last year against 92 in China and eight in Europe.
