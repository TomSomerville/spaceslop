NASA is lining up PROMISE, a south pole rover assembled largely from Mars program leftovers, for a launch in late 2028, and Blue Origin's Blue Moon Mark 1 lander has been picked to carry it to the surface. The rover is a foundational piece of NASA's Moon Base program, meant to scout and prospect ahead of any permanent outpost.

PROMISE stands for Polar Rover for Observation, Mapping, and In-Situ Exploration. NASA's page describes a six-wheeled, SUV-sized vehicle. The Jet Propulsion Laboratory is adapting electronics, body structure and mobility hardware from the Curiosity test-bed rover, along with flight-spare and test-bed parts from Curiosity and Perseverance. Space.com reported in June that the rover began as an engineering model JPL used to try out fixes and commands before sending them to the real rovers on Mars, and quoted NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman saying the agency has "hardware that the taxpayers have invested a lot in."

## Nuclear power for the dark

The rover will run on a flight-spare multi-mission radioisotope thermoelectric generator, which turns heat from radioactive decay into electricity. That matters at the south pole, where sunlight is patchy and permanently shadowed craters are the whole point. The other lunar robots in NASA's Moon Base lineup are solar powered, according to Space.com.

The primary mission is planned for 18 months. The payload includes TRIDENT, a drill built by Honeybee Robotics, plus a mass spectrometer, radiation and temperature sensors, a laser retroreflector and stereo cameras. It will also demonstrate a radiation-tolerant flight computer, lidar and autonomous navigation software. The goal is to characterize the surface and subsurface, assess water ice and other volatiles, and inform future landing site selection.

## The Blue Origin angle

Blue Origin is on both ends of this mission: Mark 1 is the ride, and Honeybee Robotics, which designed and leads the drill, is a Blue Origin subsidiary. New Space Economy reported that the delivery award, announced November 7, includes a $94 million base value and a $140 million performance-based option. NASA's own PROMISE page does not state contract values, so treat those figures as single-sourced.

The delivery follows NASA's May Moon Base announcement, in which Blue Origin received $188 million for two task orders with a $280.4 million option, covering rover deliveries to the south pole region. Mark 1 has not yet flown. Space.com noted in June that a New Glenn explosion during an engine test the previous month complicated the lander's timeline.

Using a retired test rover does carry a cost: Space.com noted it leaves Curiosity and Perseverance without an Earth-based testbed. Isaacman judged that worth it. NASA's PROMISE page was last updated November 10.
