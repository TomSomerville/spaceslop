Hubble Network has raised a $200 million Series C at a $1.6 billion valuation and said any device with a Bluetooth Low Energy radio can now connect to its satellites. The announcement, dated September 23, 2026, brings total funding to $300 million, a year after a $70 million Series B.

## What changes

The pitch is that a Bluetooth chip, already in billions of products, can talk directly to a satellite without a dedicated modem. Hubble says adopting the network needs only its SDK and a firmware update. On a new design, it says, connectivity adds under $0.50 to the bill of materials, roughly a tenth of a cellular modem's cost and a fiftieth of a satellite modem's.

The company frames the market around two gaps: cellular covers about 15% of Earth's landmass, and dedicated satellite tracking hardware costs $25 or more per device. It cites theft and loss costing the average mid-sized business $13 million a year.

## The network so far

Hubble reports six satellites in low Earth orbit today and a target of 60 by 2030. Alongside them runs a terrestrial network of more than 100 million gateways across 170 countries. More than 500,000 devices are active, which it says is over ten times the figure of a year earlier. The new money is earmarked for expanding the constellation and cutting connectivity costs.

Smith Point Capital led the round, with Seraphim, Carthona Capital, Earthshot Ventures, Y Combinator and RPM Ventures participating. Partners and customers named in the release include Samsara, Texas Instruments, InPlay, Reelables and Muon Space, which is building next-generation satellites for Hubble on its MuSat XL platform.

CEO and co-founder Alex Haro said that when the company announced its plan, "most of the industry said the physics wouldn't allow it." Keith Block, Smith Point's founder and CEO, called Hubble the builder of "the foundational infrastructure that the physical world is missing."

## Context

Hubble sits at the low-bandwidth, low-cost end of direct-to-device connectivity, far from the phone-to-satellite services that dominate headlines. Its customers care about location pings and sensor readings rather than voice or video, so a six-satellite constellation can already do useful work, though coverage gaps between passes are likely at that size. The release does not say how often satellites pass over a given spot, and the jump to 60 satellites is a plan, not a funded launch manifest.
