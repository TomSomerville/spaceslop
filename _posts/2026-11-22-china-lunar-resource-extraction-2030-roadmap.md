Wu Weiren, chief designer of China's lunar exploration program, and his research team have laid out a staged roadmap for pulling resources off the Moon, with a first technology demonstration due by 2030, according to Interesting Engineering's report on a paper due to appear in the *Journal of Deep Space Exploration*.

The proposal has three milestones. By 2030, China would validate at least one key technology for extracting water or oxygen from lunar material, flown on an upcoming robotic Chang'e mission or a planned crewed landing. By 2040 the roadmap calls for a small-scale facility producing water, oxygen and construction materials. By 2050 it envisions full-scale extraterrestrial resource extraction.

## The techniques on the table

The authors discuss mining water ice from permanently shadowed regions near the lunar poles and splitting it by electrolysis into hydrogen and oxygen. They also cover pulling oxygen out of regolith, the loose lunar soil, using molten regolith electrolysis or hydrogen reduction, and processing regolith into bricks or blocks for construction. This family of techniques is known as in-situ resource utilization, or ISRU: making propellant, air and building material on site rather than hauling it from Earth.

Beyond the Moon's surface, the paper flags the five Lagrange points and distant retrograde orbits as strategically important for communications, observation and staging. It cites the Queqiao relay satellite near the Earth-Moon L2 point, which supports the Chang'e-4 lander on the far side, as an existing example.

## Not yet national policy

The report says China does not yet have a dedicated national strategy or medium- to long-term plan for off-Earth resources. The researchers call the next two decades a critical period for moving space resource development from research to industry, and the roadmap asks for more private-sector participation and a national funding mechanism. In other words, this is a proposal from people close to the program, not an announced government commitment.

## Context

The timing lines up with China's lunar schedule as described in search coverage of the plan: Chang'e 7 is slated for 2027 and Chang'e 8 for 2028, both aimed at the south pole, with Chang'e 8 expected to test in-situ resource utilization, and a crewed landing targeted for 2029 or 2030. A 2030 demo would therefore have candidate rides, but the paper stops short of naming one.
