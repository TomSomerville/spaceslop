Japan's government is preparing to move operation of its flagship H3 rocket from the space agency JAXA to Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (MHI), with a target of completing the handover by 2027. The education ministry and JAXA are only now entering discussions with MHI, so the plan is not final, and the terms remain to be negotiated.

The trigger was a clean flight. According to a report carried by JKN, the 10th H3 launch on October 20 went off with no major abnormalities, and officials judged the program mature enough to pursue a transfer. That is a long way from March 2023, when the first H3 failed after its second-stage engine did not ignite. The second flight, in February 2024, succeeded after fixes.

## What changes

Today MHI already handles production, launch preparation and operations, but JAXA keeps final responsibility for each launch. Under the plan, MHI would take over the whole business: rocket production, launch service contracts, scheduling, launch preparation, and pre-launch checks of the rocket and satellites. Not everything moves. Launch-site functions such as tracking and flight safety will stay with public bodies, and the exact division of roles with JAXA is to be set separately.

The motive is cost and customers. Digitimes, summarizing a report originally from Nikkei, says the shift is meant to cut launch costs and make H3 a more credible commercial rival to SpaceX. JKN adds that a single company controlling both production and launches could line up parts purchasing with launch volumes and tie manufacturing to the launch schedule. H3 development began in 2014 with the goal of halving the roughly 10 billion yen per-launch cost of its predecessor, the H-IIA. The aim now is more overseas and commercial satellite orders.

## Context

There is precedent. JKN notes that this is the same path as earlier Japanese rockets, though details on those transfers were not in the material reviewed for this article. The H3 is expendable, unlike SpaceX's Falcon 9, and JAXA says it would redirect staff toward H3 improvements and research on reusable rockets once operations move to MHI.

The Digitimes piece is paywalled beyond its lead, and no timeline, contract terms or launch-rate targets beyond "by 2027" were available. Watch for the ministry's formal framework and for whether MHI wins commercial customers once it controls pricing.
