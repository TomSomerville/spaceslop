The Senate Appropriations Committee's Commerce-Justice-Science subcommittee released its fiscal 2027 bill on 12 November, proposing $24.874 billion for NASA. That is a little above the $24.438 billion enacted for FY2026 and well above the $18.8 billion the administration asked for. The House Appropriations Committee approved $24.4 billion in May on a party-line vote, so both chambers have now rejected the deep cuts in the request.

## Science and exploration

The Senate puts science at $7.43 billion, against $6 billion in the House bill and $3.9 billion in the request. It objects to the plan to end 53 missions, which the report says was driven by budget pressure rather than merit, and rejects ending the Terra, Aqua and Aura Earth-observing satellites. Planetary science gets $2.617 billion, including $500 million for New Frontiers and $430 million of that for Dragonfly.

Exploration gets $8.282 billion, $499 million above the enacted level. Highlights from the report, as summarized by SpacePolicyOnline:

- SLS: $1.495 billion, plus $1.025 billion from last year's reconciliation law, and a nudge for NASA to find a path for flights after Artemis V.
- Orion: at least $1.222 billion.
- Human Landing System: at least $2.277 billion for the two contracted landers and heavy cargo development.
- Lunar spacesuits: at least $830 million.
- Fission surface power: up to $250 million.
- Gateway: the committee approves redirecting reconciliation money from Gateway to work supporting a continuous human presence on the Moon. For the Moon Base effort, NASA must use existing CLPS and LSII capabilities, and the National Academies would review the architecture.

Space operations gets $4.125 billion. The committee urges full use of the International Space Station and provides up to $299.8 million for Commercial LEO Destinations.

## What happens next

None of the 12 FY2027 appropriations bills has cleared Congress, and the full Senate committee has announced no markup. The government is running on a continuing resolution that expires on 11 December. The subcommittee also notes that NASA's mid-2026 reorganization of its mission directorates and budget structure came too late, and with too little detail, to be reflected in the bill.
