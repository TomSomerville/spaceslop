NASA has put a date on the departure of the SpaceX Dragon that has been docked to the International Space Station since mid-October. The capsule, flying the agency's 35th commercial resupply mission, is set to undock no earlier than Wednesday, 18 November, at 11:05 a.m. EST, weather permitting, according to a NASA release.

The undocking comes from the Harmony module's space-facing port, and NASA's live coverage begins at 10:45 a.m. EST on its website. Splashdown is targeted off the California coast no earlier than 1:50 a.m. EST on Thursday, 19 November (10:50 p.m. PST on 18 November). NASA says it will not stream the splashdown itself, and will post timing updates on the station blog instead.

This is the date that was missing from our [earlier report](/2026/11/13/iss-dragon-return-prep/) on the crew packing the spacecraft, when NASA had not yet announced when the capsule would leave. Recovery conditions can still move the schedule, and NASA's release notes that splashdown timing depends on the weather in the recovery area.

## What is coming home

The Dragon launched on a Falcon 9 from Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, Florida, and reached the station on 15 October with more than 6,300 pounds of crew supplies, science investigations and equipment. NASA lists thousands of pounds of cargo for the trip down, including a 3D-bioprinted blood vessel construct for studying vascular aging and cardiovascular disease, and frozen tumor organoids treated aboard the station for cancer-treatment research.

Also aboard are frozen microbial samples for work on how antibiotic-resistant bacteria survive and evolve in space, and a lighting system that tested its potential to support astronaut sleep and wellbeing, returning after more than three years on the station.

## Why it matters

Dragon remains the main way the station gets bulky science samples back to Earth in good condition, since cold-stored biological material needs a soft ocean landing and a fast recovery. SpaceX controllers at its Hawthorne, California, facility command the undocking, with NASA coordinating station operations. A release this routine rarely makes headlines, but the quick turnaround from splashdown to lab is the point: researchers get their samples within hours rather than weeks.
