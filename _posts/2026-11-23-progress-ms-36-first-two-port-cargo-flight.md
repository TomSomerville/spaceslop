Russia's Progress MS-36 cargo ship is scheduled to lift off from Baikonur on a Soyuz-2.1b on Nov. 24, and it will do something no Progress has done before: use two different docking ports on the International Space Station during one mission.

RussianSpaceWeb reports the launch from Site 31 is planned for 13:13 Moscow time (5:13 a.m. EST), though the text of its report says the time was adjusted to 13:17. Docking is planned at the zenith port of the Poisk module, with the date given as Nov. 26 by RussianSpaceWeb. Other listings point to Nov. 25, so the exact day is worth rechecking closer to launch. The ship carries roughly 2,500 kilograms of cargo, including food and other supplies, plus propellant, water and oxygen for the station.

## Working around the Zvezda leak

The unusual part is what happens after docking. Traditionally Progress ships unloaded through the hatch at the aft end of the Zvezda service module. That hatch to Zvezda's transfer chamber, known as the PrK, is permanently closed because of an air leak that has resisted repair. According to RussianSpaceWeb, MS-36 will instead be unloaded at Poisk, then undock and redock at Zvezda's aft port. There the freighter's engines line up with the station's main axis, so it can fire them for the periodic orbit-raising burns the ISS needs. The report says this two-step routine is expected to become standard for Progress-MS flights until the station is deorbited.

## A flight that slipped and went quiet

MS-36 was first planned for Aug. 17, 2026, then pushed to Sept. 9. The collapse of the service platform at Baikonur's Site 31 in November 2025 caused knock-on delays, and the mission was rescheduled for late November. RussianSpaceWeb also notes that Roscosmos largely stopped giving timely updates on the campaign from around June 2026, and that NASA made no comment on the mission. SpacePolicyOnline lists the launch with a question mark, reflecting that uncertainty.

The ship is the fourth and last Russian resupply flight of 2026 and is expected to stay at the station for about six months, according to RussianSpaceWeb, which flags that figure as uncertain.

Why it matters: the Zvezda leak has been a long-running concern on the Russian segment, and this profile shows Russia adapting operations around it rather than fixing it. Rerouting cargo away from the PrK keeps reboost capability intact, which the station depends on to stay in orbit.
