The UK Space Agency published its Technical Framework on Lunar Operations on 23 November, setting out how British-licensed missions to the Moon should handle disposal, registration, safety and liability. According to Tech Times, which reported on the document, the framework creates no new law. The text itself says it "does not create new legal requirements." Instead it explains how existing duties under the Outer Space Act 1986 and the Space Industry Act 2018 apply to lunar work, and the Civil Aviation Authority will take it into account when assessing licence applications.

## What it covers

The framework applies to four kinds of mission: cislunar operations, Moon-orbiting missions, static landers and rovers. The headline requirement is that a licensed lunar mission must have a post-mission disposal baseline in place when its licence is granted, and operators must report the final disposal state and location. As described, that applies to missions that had not passed a Concept Design Review, or an equivalent stage agreed with the CAA, by 23 November. The text reportedly does not define that review, and it is unclear what applies to missions already past it.

Where a disposal plan involves a surface impact, it must avoid crewed surface operations and, where feasible, permanently shadowed regions and polar areas. Operators must also supply lunar orbit parameters or surface location details and share ephemeris data.

## Safety zones and liability

The UK rejects keep-out zones around lunar activity. It will consider temporary, proportionate notification zones that comply with Articles I, II and IX of the Outer Space Treaty, although critics argue any exclusion conflicts with Article II's ban on national appropriation.

On money, liability and insurance requirements are waived for lunar missions launched before the end of 2030, covering the orbital phase for licences issued from 1 December. A separate Liability and Insurance Technical Framework is due by December 2026, and the waiver will be reviewed in 2029. Nuclear power and space resources are handled case by case, and spectrum remains a matter for Ofcom.

## The upper-stage gap

The notable omission is rocket upper stages. The framework does not address launch vehicles or say who is responsible for spent stages sent toward the Moon. Tech Times contrasts that with the FCC's new cislunar licence category in the US, which likewise carries no disposal requirement for spent trans-lunar injection stages. For an industry that is about to fly many more lunar missions, the stage left behind after the burn remains a regulatory blind spot on both sides of the Atlantic.

Several decisions are expected in 2027, including the conclusion of the UN space resources working group and a review of the framework after the ITU conference.

Tech Times is the only outlet whose coverage of the document's contents was available for this report, so details here rest on that single account. The framework is listed on the UK Space Agency's GOV.UK page as a publication dated 23 November 2026.
