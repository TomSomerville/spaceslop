The White House has asked Congress to carve $2 billion for Artemis out of the next stopgap spending bill. The request went up on Monday, Nov. 23, as one line in a 12-page list of "anomalies": exceptions to the usual freeze at last year's funding level that a continuing resolution imposes.

According to a document photographed by Eric Berger, the money would pay for "supply chain acceleration via in-house manufacturing" of NASA-owned hardware, including spacesuits, thrusters, SLS components, habitats and crew safety systems. Without it, the document says, "activities would be delayed, missing the required operational need date." The reporting does not say which Artemis budget lines would receive the cash. An unnamed NASA official told Berger the funding would let the agency do more, not less, but NASA has not confirmed that on the record.

## A request, not a budget

The $2 billion is an exception to a freeze, not an appropriation. NASA is currently running at its fiscal 2026 level of $24.4 billion under the existing stopgap, which expires Dec. 11. The administration's fiscal 2027 request still stands at $18.8 billion for NASA, including $8.5 billion for Artemis, a 23% cut from the enacted level. The Senate's draft would fund the agency at $24.87 billion.

Artemis is small change on the list. The same document asks for up to $5 billion at an annualized rate for the Golden Dome missile shield, with the White House saying Golden Dome "will be unable to continue production" without it, plus about $21.3 billion in annualized increases for Navy submarine programs and authority to start buying materials for a new battleship class. One outlet reported the list assumes a stopgap running through March 31.

## Why the schedule matters

The program is under schedule pressure. Berger's account puts Artemis II's crewed lunar flyby in April 2026, with Artemis III now a 14-day docking test in low Earth orbit in 2027 and the first crewed landing moved to Artemis IV in 2028. Ars Technica reported on Nov. 19 that Orion was not designed to fly backward during lunar transit to dock with commercial landers, and that its European Service Module needs modifications.

Nothing here is settled. No member of Congress had publicly backed the Artemis item at the time of reporting, and Congress returns next week with Democrats set to take both chambers in January, which makes this the last funding bill the current Congress is expected to write.
