Guoke Xingqing Aerospace Technology, a Chinese electric-propulsion startup, disclosed on 9 November that it has completed several financing rounds adding up to nearly 100 million yuan, according to a 36Kr report. The company did not give a precise figure or valuation.

The investors named include a Shanghai Science and Technology Group strategic fund, Hangzhou Science and Technology Group, Nanshan Strategic Emerging Industry Investment, Xingxiang Science and Technology, Guotai Haitong and New Micro Capital. The money is earmarked for automated production lines and development of new propulsion products.

## What it builds

The company makes Hall-effect thrusters, a type of electric engine that ionises a gas and accelerates it with electric and magnetic fields. Its range covers thrust from micronewtons up to 300 millinewtons, meant for platforms from micro-satellites to spacecraft in the tonne class. It runs on krypton, xenon or argon; krypton is favoured for large constellations because it is cheaper than xenon. Key parts, including hollow cathodes, are developed in-house.

Guoke Xingqing was incubated by the Hangzhou Institute for Advanced Study of the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences, where the core team came from. The founder is named as Xu Luxiang.

## Flight heritage and scale-up

According to the report, dozens of the company's units are operating in orbit, including what it describes as the first engineering-scale use of krypton Hall thrusters in a satellite-internet orbit at roughly 1,000 km. Customers reportedly include major commercial satellite makers and two national low-orbit broadband constellations. These claims come from the company via 36Kr and were not independently verified.

The company is building a line able to produce 1,000 Hall propulsion systems a year, with production expected to begin in December. Longer term it plans to move into high-power propulsion, very-low-orbit propulsion, in-orbit servicing and debris removal.

The raise is small by Western standards but fits a pattern in China's commercial space sector: suppliers of components such as thrusters are being funded to meet demand from the megaconstellations now being deployed, where thousands of satellites each need a propulsion system.
