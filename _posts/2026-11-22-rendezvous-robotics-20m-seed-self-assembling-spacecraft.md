Rendezvous Robotics, a Golden, Colorado startup building spacecraft that put themselves together in orbit, has closed a $20 million seed round, according to a report published October 29. The round was oversubscribed and is the company's first priced financing.

MaC Venture Capital and Toyota Ventures led it. The University of Michigan Endowment, Astera and Industrious Ventures also participated, as did existing backers Aurelia Foundry, ATX Venture Partners and Mana Ventures.

## What it builds

The company's concept is a spacecraft made of modular tiles, each roughly a metre across and hexagonal, carrying sensors and onboard processing. The tiles use electromagnetic actuation to move relative to each other, navigate to a rendezvous point and dock without outside robotic help. Operators could add tiles for computing, solar power, propulsion, communications or thermal control, and rearrange them after launch. That also opens the door to repair or reconfiguration in orbit rather than building and launching a new vehicle.

The approach descends from nearly a decade of TESSERAE research. Ariel Ekblaw developed the original self-assembly work at MIT, and it later continued at the Aurelia Institute, which she founded. Earlier TESSERAE generations have flown on Blue Origin's New Shepard and on two International Space Station missions. Joe Landon is co-founder and CEO of Rendezvous Robotics.

## Milestones

The roadmap is staged and, so far, mostly near the ground:

- **November 2026:** a parabolic flight campaign to test electromagnetic actuation and mobility in microgravity.
- **December 2026:** a TESSERAE demonstration on the ISS in which 32 tiles assemble into a three-dimensional structure in a controlled environment.
- **No earlier than April 2028:** a low Earth orbit demonstration in which seven independently deployed tiles connect into one spacecraft, separate, and reassemble in a different configuration.

## Why it matters

Satellites are limited by what fits inside a fairing. In-space assembly, whether by robotic arms or by self-assembling parts as here, is one way around that, and it matters for large power and communications systems in particular. Landon's pitch, as the report describes it, is spacecraft with more power generation and bigger communications capacity than a single launch can carry.

The caveat is that free-flying autonomous assembly has not yet been shown in real orbit; the ISS test happens inside a controlled environment. The 2028 demo is where the claim gets tested, and a seed round of this size should cover the path to it.
